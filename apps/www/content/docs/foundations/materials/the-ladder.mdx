## Overview

Six rungs, ordered by how much separation the surface claims from what is behind
it. The first three are opaque and the last three are translucent, and that
boundary — between rung 2 and rung 3 — is the most important line in the system,
because everything above it is subject to
[the contrast floor](./the-contrast-floor.mdx) and everything below it is not.

<MaterialLadder />

The demonstration stacks all six over a deliberately hostile photographic
backdrop, with the scrim and reduced-transparency toggles live, so that the
floor is something you can see rather than something this page asserts.

## How it works

### The six rungs

| # | Name | Opaque? | Blur | Its job |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | `ground` | yes | — | the page itself; the thing everything else sits on |
| 1 | `panel` | yes | — | an inline grouping: a list section, a form block, a card that is not interactive |
| 2 | `raised` | yes | — | a thing lifted off the page: a tappable card, a menu, a popover, a toast |
| 3 | `bar` | no | small | persistent edge chrome that content scrolls **under**: nav bar, tab bar, toolbar, sticky summary |
| 4 | `overlay` | no | large | something that takes over: a sheet, a dialog, a full-screen panel |
| 5 | `scrim` | no | minimal | the dimming layer that isolates rung 4 from everything below it |

The names are the vocabulary. Use them in design review, in commit messages and
in component props; the numeric tokens exist because CSS custom properties
cannot be tidily namespaced and because the ordering has to be machine-readable.

### The four properties, rung by rung

Each rung is exactly `--opsin-material-<n>-bg`, `-blur`, `-border` and
`-shadow`. The shape of the ladder is easier to see as a set of movements than
as a table of values:

- **0 → 1** adds a border and changes the backing from the page colour to the
  card colour. No shadow. A `panel` is a *region*, not an object.
- **1 → 2** adds the only ordinary shadow in the system. Rung 2 is where a
  surface becomes an object you could pick up.
- **2 → 3** is the boundary. The backing gains an alpha, a blur appears, and the
  border becomes a hairline expressed in alpha rather than a solid neutral. The
  shadow shrinks almost to nothing, because a bar's separation comes from the
  blur, not from a drop shadow.
- **3 → 4** lowers the alpha slightly and raises the blur substantially, and
  brings back a large soft shadow. An `overlay` is further from the page than a
  `bar` and has to look it.
- **4 → 5** is a different kind of thing entirely: `scrim` is a dark wash with
  almost no blur, whose job is to *remove* information rather than to present
  any.

### Blur, tint, saturation and vibrancy

Four things are commonly bundled into "frosted glass". opsinjs uses two of them
and deliberately declines the other two.

**Tint — used.** The backing colour and its alpha. This is the safety parameter:
it is what bounds the composite and therefore what makes contrast measurable.
See [The contrast floor](./the-contrast-floor.mdx).

**Blur — used, for a narrow reason.** `backdrop-filter: blur()` removes high
spatial frequency from what is behind the surface. It takes the *texture* out of
a photograph so that glyph edges are not competing with edges behind them. It
does **not** meaningfully change the local mean luminance, so it does not fix
contrast, and it is never traded against tint.

**Saturation boost — not used.** Boosting the saturation of the backdrop is what
gives platform materials their characteristic liveliness. We decline it because
it changes the backdrop's colour in a direction we cannot bound: the composite is
no longer a simple interpolation between the tint and the backdrop, and the
worst-case calculation stops being valid. If it is ever added it will be a
per-rung token defaulting to no boost, so that the default remains measurable.

**Vibrancy — not used.** Platform vibrancy blends the *foreground* text with the
backdrop as well, which is a beautiful effect and an unbounded contrast
liability. Text on an opsinjs material is opaque, always.

### Browser support, honestly

`backdrop-filter: blur()` is safe to use everywhere a modern browser runs, and
has been for years. Two caveats that are still real:

- Older Safari needs the `-webkit-` prefix, which costs one line and is emitted.
- `backdrop-filter` with an **SVG filter reference** — `backdrop-filter:
  url(#something)` — is a Chromium-only capability. Nothing in this ladder uses
  it, and nothing in this ladder ever will, because a rung that only exists in
  one engine is not a rung.

Where `backdrop-filter` is unavailable entirely, rungs 3, 4 and 5 land on their
opaque fallbacks — the same fallbacks that reduced transparency and print use.
That path is exercised by real readers, not just by a feature query, so it is
designed rather than tolerated.

## Using it

**Say the rung's name.** A design review conversation that contains "make it a
bit more glassy" produces a screen with four different alphas. One that contains
"that should be `bar`, not `overlay`" produces a screen with two.

**The boundary between rung 2 and rung 3 is a decision, not a gradient.** Ask:
does content scroll under this, or does it sit on the page? Chrome that content
passes beneath is `bar`. Anything else that needs to look lifted is `raised`,
opaque, with a shadow. There is no half-translucent card.

**A toast is `raised`, not `bar`.** A toast appears over arbitrary content it
knows nothing about, frequently over a photograph or a chart, and it usually
carries a message that matters. It must not depend on what is behind it, so it
is opaque.

**Rung 5 never appears without rung 4.** A scrim exists to isolate an overlay. A
dimmed screen with nothing on top of it is a loading state pretending to be a
modal.

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    A results list on `ground`, sections grouped as `panel`, each tappable
    reading as `raised`, a sticky summary bar as `bar`, and a detail sheet as
    `overlay` over a `scrim`. Five rungs on one screen, at most three of them
    composited at any moment.
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    Every card given the `bar` material because the frosted look reads as
    premium. Each card is now a compositing layer, each one's contrast depends on
    the row of the photograph behind it, and the sticky bar has lost the only
    visual property that distinguished it from the content.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

**Do not add a rung.** If a surface does not fit one of the six, the usual cause
is that it is trying to be two things — a card that is also chrome, a sheet that
is also a page. Splitting it is almost always the right fix; the intake process
for a genuine seventh rung is in
[Contributing tokens](../../handbook/contributing/contributing-tokens.mdx).

## Accessibility impact

**The opaque rungs (0, 1, 2) carry the ordinary obligation.** Their backgrounds
are known colours, so text on them is measured exactly as any other pair is —
see [Contrast and APCA](../colour/contrast-and-apca.mdx).

**The translucent rungs (3, 4, 5) carry a stronger one.** Their rendered colour
depends on the backdrop, so they are measured against the worst-case composite
rather than against their nominal tint. Every one of them clears the floor
against a pure-black and a pure-white backdrop, in both themes, which is what
makes them safe over imagery and video. The calculation is
[The contrast floor](./the-contrast-floor.mdx).

**Borders are measured too.** A hairline at low alpha over a translucent rung is
the most commonly missed non-text contrast failure (SC 1.4.11), because it looks
correct on the one backdrop the designer tested.

**The fallback path is a first-class path.** Reduced transparency is not a
degraded experience — the rung keeps its border, its shadow and its position, and
only its translucency changes, so nothing moves and nothing is lost.
[Reduced transparency](./reduced-transparency.mdx) states it rung by rung.
