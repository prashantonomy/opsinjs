## What this means

Design principles fail in review because they are agreeable. "Be clear", "be
calm", "do not alarm" — nobody argues, nobody changes anything, and the screen
ships. A principle that cannot be violated cannot be checked.

So the ten rules below are written as prohibitions with an observable subject.
Each one names a thing that must not appear on screen, and each one can be
answered yes or no by someone holding a phone who has never met the team. They
are the short form of this entire section: every rule expands into a page, and
every page exists because the rule needed an argument behind it.

They are also the source of the
[Safety review checklist](./safety-review-checklist.mdx), which is the same list
in a form you can print and tick.

## The rule

**These ten prohibitions apply to every screen that displays, interprets or
collects health information about the person using it.**

<SafetyCallout severity="attention" evidence="opinion">
  A screen that violates any one of these is not a style problem to fix later.
  Each of the ten describes a way a competent, attractive interface misleads
  someone about their own body.
</SafetyCallout>

### 1. Never carry clinical status in colour alone

Colour is one channel among four. Status must survive greyscale, protanopia and a
sunlit screen, which means colour plus an icon plus a word, always.
→ [The two colour axes](./two-colour-axes.mdx),
[Colour independence](../accessibility/colour-independence.mdx)

### 2. Never mix the category axis with the status axis

A colour that identifies a metric family may not also connote severity, and a
severity colour may not identify a family. Two axes, two palettes, no overlap.
→ [The two colour axes](./two-colour-axes.mdx)

### 3. Never use the word "normal" for a person's own result

"Normal" is a statement about a population that reads as a statement about a
person, and its negation is unbearable. Say "in the usual range", "above the
usual range", or name the range directly.
→ [Reference ranges](./reference-ranges.mdx)

### 4. Never show a number without its unit, its scale and its recency

A bare figure is not information. Unit, the range it sits in, and when it was
measured are part of the value, not decoration around it.
→ [Numbers, units and precision](./numbers-units-precision.mdx),
[Uncertainty, staleness and missing data](./uncertainty-and-staleness.mdx)

### 5. Never present more precision than the measurement carries

Rounding is a truth claim. A weight rendered as `71.4396 kg`, or a
device-estimated figure printed to two decimals, asserts an accuracy that does
not exist.
→ [Numbers, units and precision](./numbers-units-precision.mdx),
[Data provenance and device accuracy](./data-provenance-and-device-accuracy.mdx)

### 6. Never draw a trend from fewer points than the metric requires

Two readings are two readings, not a direction. Every metric has a minimum window
below which a trend line is a lie with a nice curve.
→ [Trends and change](./trends-and-change.mdx)

### 7. Never allow more than one `urgent` surface on a screen

Urgency has a budget. The second red thing does not double the attention paid; it
halves it, and it teaches the reader that red is decorative.
→ [Alarm fatigue](./alarm-fatigue.mdx)

### 8. Never convey urgency with motion, sound or haptics alone

A pulsing element is invisible under reduced motion, invisible to someone
scrolling past, and unavailable to a screen reader. Motion may support a message
it never carries.
→ [Motion in health UI](./motion-in-health-ui.mdx),
[Notifications and off-screen alerts](./notifications-and-off-screen-alerts.mdx)

### 9. Never present a result without saying what, if anything, to do

Every clinical statement needs an action slot, even when the action is "nothing,
and here is when we will look again". An unresolved result is answered by the
reader's own worst guess.
→ [Delivering difficult results](./delivering-difficult-results.mdx),
[Emergency and escalation](./emergency-and-escalation.mdx)

### 10. Never collect health data without saying what it is for and how to stop

Consent is per-purpose, revocable in the place the data appears, and re-asked
when the purpose changes. Burying it in onboarding is not consent, it is a
formality.
→ [Consent and disclosure](./consent-and-disclosure.mdx)

## Why (evidence)

<ResearchNote evidence="mixed" date="2026-09-02">
  Rules 1 and 8 are grounded in normative accessibility requirements rather than
  our preference. WCAG 2.2 success criterion 1.4.1 (Use of Colour) forbids colour
  as the only visual means of conveying information, and 2.2.2 (Pause, Stop,
  Hide) constrains moving content. Those are checkable against a published
  standard, and we hold ourselves to them; see
  [Accessibility](../accessibility/index.mdx) for how we test.

  Rules 3, 4 and 5 draw on the published work on how laypeople read test
  results — in particular the systematic review of laboratory-result presentation
  formats in JMIR (2024), [10.2196/53993](https://doi.org/10.2196/53993),
  and the earlier patient-portal presentation study in BMC Medical Informatics
  and Decision Making (2018),
  [10.1186/s12911-018-0589-7](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12911-018-0589-7).
  We cite these as the anchors for "a number needs a scale", not as proof of any
  specific visual treatment.

  Rules 2, 6, 7, 9 and 10 are **opinion**: engineering and design judgement about
  what keeps a system coherent as it grows, argued in full on the page each rule
  links to. Rule 7's "one urgent per screen" in particular is a budget we chose,
  not a measured threshold, and we say so plainly on
  [Alarm fatigue](./alarm-fatigue.mdx).
</ResearchNote>

## Applying it

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    Run the ten as a pre-merge question list on any screen that touches health
    data, the way you would run a security review. Ten yes/no answers takes
    minutes.
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    Treat them as aspirations in a slide deck. A prohibition that is never
    checked is a preference.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    Push the enforceable subset into tooling. The never-mix-the-axes rule and the
    banned-word rule are both lint rules, not review conversations; see
    [Tooling](../handbook/tooling/index.mdx).
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    Rely on review alone for a rule a machine can check. Human review should be
    spent on the seven rules a machine cannot.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    When you must break one, write down which rule, why, who agreed, and what
    compensating control you added. A recorded exception is a decision.
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    Break one silently because the design looked better. That is how a system
    loses the property that made it worth adopting.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

## What this does not cover

- **The thresholds themselves.** Nothing here tells you which value is
  `attention`. That is clinical content and it is yours.
- **Clinical safety process.** Hazard logs, clinical risk management and the
  standards that govern them are summarised at
  [Regulatory context](./regulatory-context.mdx) and are a process obligation on
  your organisation, not a UI rule.
- **Clinician-facing interfaces**, where several of these rules invert. Density
  and absolute values beat reassurance for a trained reader.
- **Wording.** These rules constrain structure; the sentences themselves are
  governed by [Content and language](../content/index.mdx).
