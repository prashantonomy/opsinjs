## What this means

A dashboard shows four tiles. Blood pressure, heart rate, sleep and weight. They
look identical: same card, same typography, same authority.

Behind them: the blood pressure came from a validated upper-arm cuff. The heart
rate is a wrist optical estimate, which is good at rest and much less good during
movement. The sleep figure is a model's inference from movement and heart rate,
not a measurement of sleep at all. The weight was typed in by the user, from
memory, last Thursday.

Four completely different epistemic objects, rendered as one kind of thing. The
reader has no way to know that one of these numbers is a measurement and another
is a guess made by an algorithm about a process it cannot observe, and the
interface has quietly conferred the credibility of the first onto the fourth.

Provenance is not metadata. It is the part of the value that says how much weight
to put on it, and dropping it is a category error rather than an omission.

## The rule

**Every health value carries its provenance, and what the interface may assert
about a value is bounded by that provenance.**

**Four provenance classes**, in descending order of what the interface may claim:

| Class | Example | The interface may |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Clinically measured** | Laboratory result, clinic-measured blood pressure | Compare against an attributed reference range; carry a status |
| **Device measured** | Validated home cuff, glucose meter, scale | Compare against a range where the device's method supports it; carry a status where a clinical rule says so |
| **Device estimated** | Optical heart rate in motion, sleep stages, energy expenditure | Show as an estimate, show trends; **never** carry a clinical status on its own |
| **Self-reported** | Typed weight, symptom rating, remembered dose | Record and display as reported; never present as measured |

**Rules:**

1. **Provenance is recorded at capture and travels with the value.** It cannot be
   reconstructed later and it cannot be defaulted.
2. **Provenance is visible to the reader**, in the place the number is, in plain
   words: "from your cuff", "estimated by your watch", "you entered this".
3. **A value's status is bounded by its class.** An estimated value may not
   escalate on its own. It may prompt a measurement: "Your watch noticed
   something unusual — take a reading with your cuff."
4. **Never mix classes in one figure without saying so.** A weekly average
   combining two measured and five self-reported readings is a different object
   from an average of seven measurements.
5. **Name the device, not the brand's marketing.** "Your Model X cuff" is
   provenance; "clinically validated technology" is advertising.
6. **Do not restate a manufacturer's accuracy claim as your own.** If you cite
   one, attribute it and link it. Never paraphrase it into a stronger claim, and
   never invent a figure.
7. **Say what a device does not do.** A wrist sensor does not measure blood
   pressure; a movement-based sleep model does not measure sleep stages. Naming
   the limitation once, where the number appears, prevents a great deal of
   misplaced trust.
8. **A model output is labelled as a model output**, with what it takes as input.
   See [Risk and statistics](./risk-and-statistics.mdx).
9. **Self-reported data is never silently corrected.** Question implausible
   entries; do not overwrite them.
10. **Exports carry provenance.** A clinician receiving a summary needs to know
    which numbers are measurements. See
    [Sharing with a clinician](../patterns/sharing-with-a-clinician.mdx).

<SafetyCallout severity="attention" evidence="opinion">
  Rule 3 is the one with teeth. The most common serious provenance failure in
  consumer health products is an estimated value driving an alert — a wrist
  sensor's reading during exercise raising a cardiac alarm, or a movement-based
  sleep model triggering advice. The estimate is not wrong; it is being asked a
  question it cannot answer.
</SafetyCallout>

## Why (evidence)

<ResearchNote evidence="opinion" date="2026-09-02">
  Opinion, and deliberately unquantified.

  We could fill this page with device accuracy figures. We will not, for two
  reasons. First, accuracy is method-specific and condition-specific: a sensor
  that performs well at rest on one skin tone and one wrist position may perform
  materially worse under other conditions, and a single headline figure hides
  exactly the variation that matters. Second, published figures date quickly and
  a stale number copied from a design system into a product is a provenance
  failure of our own. Any accuracy claim in your product should come from the
  manufacturer's current documentation or from a study you have read, attributed
  in place.

  The four-class model is ours. It is coarse on purpose: a finer taxonomy would be
  more accurate and would not survive contact with a product team. Four classes
  can be assigned correctly by someone who is not an expert, which is the property
  we optimised for.

  The equity dimension is real and worth stating even though we cite nothing for
  it here: optical sensing depends on light interacting with skin, and performance
  differences across skin tones have been discussed extensively in both the
  research literature and the regulatory conversation. If your product relies on
  optical measurement, that is a question to ask your supplier explicitly, and a
  reason to prefer rule 7's honesty about limits.

  What would change our mind: nothing structural. We would happily add a fifth
  class for clinician-entered-but-not-measured data if products needed it.
</ResearchNote>

## Applying it

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    "128/82 mmHg — from your cuff, this morning at 07:40." Class, device and time
    in the same breath as the value.
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    "128/82 mmHg" with the source visible only by tapping through to a detail
    screen. Nobody taps through to check where a number came from.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    "Your watch estimated 7 hours of sleep. It works this out from movement and
    heart rate, so it is a good guide rather than an exact measure."
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    "You had 1 h 12 m of deep sleep." Presented as a measurement, to the minute,
    of something the device infers.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    "Your watch noticed a high reading during exercise. Optical readings are less
    reliable when you are moving — take a reading at rest to check."
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    Raise an `urgent` cardiac alert from a wrist optical reading taken mid-run.
    Correct data, impossible question, frightened user.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    "You entered this on 3 March." Self-reported, dated, and clearly not claimed
    as a measurement.
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    Style a typed weight identically to a scale reading. The interface has
    laundered a recollection into a measurement.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

## What this does not cover

- **Device accuracy figures.** None are published here, by design.
- **Device selection, procurement or regulatory clearance**, which are product
  decisions well outside a design system.
- **Data provenance in the technical sense** — lineage, audit trails,
  reproducibility. This page is about what the reader is told.
- **Interoperability standards.** Mapping to a clinical data standard is your
  data layer's problem; the four classes above are a presentation model, not a
  schema.
- **Freshness and staleness**, which are
  [Uncertainty, staleness and missing data](./uncertainty-and-staleness.mdx).
