## What this means

This is the canonical page for how a number is rendered in opsinjs. Where any
other page in this documentation discusses number formatting, it defers here.

Numbers in a health interface are not display strings. Each one is an assertion
about the world with an implied accuracy, and the implied accuracy is set by how
many digits you print. `71 kg` says you know someone's weight to about a
kilogram. `71.4 kg` says you know it to a hundred grams. `71.4396 kg` says you
own a laboratory balance and the person was weighed naked in a temperature-
controlled room. Only one of those three is likely to be true, and the third one
is what you get by default when a float meets a template literal.

The other half of the problem is that a number without a unit is not a number. In
health, the same measurement carries different values in different unit systems,
and some of those values overlap in plausible ranges — which means a missing or
wrong unit does not look wrong. It looks like a different, believable reading.

## The rule

**Every displayed health value is formatted by an explicit rule that states its
precision, its unit and its locale. There is no default float rendering
anywhere.**

### Precision

1. **Never display more precision than the source carries.** Precision travels
   with the measurement from its source; it is not chosen at render time to make
   a column line up.
2. **Precision is a property of the metric, not of the value.** The same metric
   is shown to the same number of decimal places every time, so a reader can
   compare two readings at a glance without re-parsing.
3. **Round half away from zero, once, at the point of display.** Never round an
   already-rounded value; never round before a comparison against a threshold.
4. **Derived and estimated values are rounded harder than measured ones**, and
   are labelled as estimates. See
   [Data provenance and device accuracy](./data-provenance-and-device-accuracy.mdx).
5. **Never pad with false zeros.** If the source gives `5`, show `5` or the
   metric's fixed `5.0`, but never `5.00` because a neighbouring metric has two
   decimals.

### Units

6. **A value is never displayed without its unit** — not in a tile, not in a
   chart tooltip, not in a table header where the number sits in the cell
   underneath, and not in an accessible name.
7. **The unit is part of the value, visually and semantically.** It does not wrap
   away from its number and it is not styled as a footnote.
8. **The unit is spoken.** `mmHg` is announced as "millimetres of mercury",
   `mmol/L` as "millimoles per litre". A screen reader reading "one twenty over
   eighty em em aitch gee" is a failure.
9. **Never convert silently.** A converted value says so, and the unit system is
   the user's choice; see [Unit systems](./unit-systems.mdx).

### Composition and locale

10. **Use the reader's locale for the decimal separator and grouping**, and never
    hand-roll it. A comma decimal separator misread as a thousands separator is a
    hundredfold error.
11. **Non-decimal composite values keep their conventional form.** Blood pressure
    is `118/76`, not two fields. Height in feet and inches is one value.
12. **Ranges use an en dash with spaces** and repeat the unit only once:
    `3.9 – 5.6 mmol/L`.
13. **Zero, none and unknown are three different things.** `0 steps` is a
    measurement. "No readings yet" is an absence. "Not available" is a failure.
    Never render an absence as `0` or as an em dash with no explanation.
14. **Negative and out-of-scale values are shown, not clamped.** Clamping a value
    to the end of a gauge hides the very case that most needs attention.
15. **Numerals are always digits, never words.** "You have 3 readings" beats
    "You have three readings" for a scanning reader, and it beats it decisively
    for a reader with low numeracy who is looking for the digit.

### Typography

16. **Use tabular figures for any number that appears in a column or updates in
    place.** Proportional figures make a list of values shimmer and make two
    readings hard to compare.
17. **The value is the largest thing in its container.** A number that must be
    hunted for is a number that will be misread.

## Why (evidence)

<ResearchNote evidence="mixed" date="2026-09-02">
  **Cited.** That presentation format changes lay comprehension of results is
  supported by the systematic review of laboratory-result presentation formats in
  JMIR, 2024, [10.2196/53993](https://doi.org/10.2196/53993). We cite it for the
  general claim and not for any specific rule on this page.

  **Opinion, but close to arithmetic.** Rules 1 to 5 are a restatement of
  significant-figure discipline, which is not a design opinion so much as a
  convention borrowed from measurement science. The design decision is only
  *where* it is enforced: we insist it is enforced at the component boundary,
  because every team that has left it to callers has ended up with the same
  metric shown to three different precisions in three different screens.

  Rule 10 is the one with the largest real-world consequence and the least
  glamour. A decimal comma rendered into a locale that reads it as a thousands
  separator turns `1,5` into `15`. For a medication dose that is a tenfold error.
  We have not seen this quantified in a consumer app and we cite nothing; we
  regard the mechanism as sufficient.

  Rule 15 conflicts with most general style guides, which prefer words for small
  numbers. We deviate deliberately for a scanning, low-numeracy reader.

  What would change our mind on rule 15: evidence that digits in running prose
  harm reading comprehension more than they help scanning.
</ResearchNote>

## Applying it

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    `118/76 mmHg` — one composite value, conventional form, unit once, announced
    as "118 over 76 millimetres of mercury".
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    `Systolic: 118.0 · Diastolic: 76.0` — two fields, false precision, and a
    reader who has to reassemble the reading they already know how to read.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    `71.4 kg` from a scale that reports to 100 g, every time, in every screen.
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    `71.4 kg` on the dashboard, `71 kg` in the summary and `71.44 kg` in the
    export. The reader cannot tell whether they lost weight or the app changed
    its mind.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    "No readings yet — log your first one to start." An absence with a next step.
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    `0 mmol/L`. A physiologically impossible value rendered as a fact, produced by
    a nullish default.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    Show an out-of-scale value at its real magnitude with the scale annotated as
    exceeded: "218 mmHg — above the top of this scale."
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    Pin the dot to the end of the bar. The reader sees "at the top of the range"
    where the truth is "far beyond it", which is the one case that mattered.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

## Components that implement this

{/* Generated from `implements`. Do not restate the list by hand. */}

`Value` is the enforcement point: it is specified to require a unit and a
precision rule rather than accepting a pre-formatted string, so a raw
`toFixed(2)` cannot reach the screen through it. Every other component that
displays a number is specified to render it through `Value` rather than
formatting inline.
