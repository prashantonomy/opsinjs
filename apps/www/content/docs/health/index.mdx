## What this means

A generic component library will happily render the number `212` in red inside a
rounded rectangle. It has no opinion about whether that number is a cholesterol
reading, whether red means "bad" or "cardiovascular", whether the person looking
at it has any idea what the unit is, or whether the app has just told someone
they are ill in a colour they cannot distinguish, at 11pm, with no way to ask a
question until Monday.

Health is the domain where the gap between "renders correctly" and "is safe to
ship" is widest. The failure mode is not a broken layout. It is a person who
misreads a result they were not prepared for and either panics or, far more
dangerous, is reassured when they should not have been. Every rule in this
section exists because a plausible, well-built, good-looking interface can
produce that outcome without anything appearing to go wrong.

This pillar is the part of opsinjs that a component library cannot be. It is
doctrine: a small number of decisions, argued in public, that constrain what the
components are allowed to do. The components are downstream of it. If you read
only one section of this documentation before writing code, read this one — it
is also, deliberately, the section that is complete today while the components
are not.

## The rule

**opsinjs decides how health information is presented. It never decides what
that information means.**

That single line is the boundary, and it cuts in both directions.

What opsinjs decides for you, and will not let you undo casually:

- **Two colour axes that never mix.** Category identity and clinical status are
  independent channels with independent palettes. A colour that says "this is a
  heart metric" can never also say "this is worrying". See
  [The two colour axes](./two-colour-axes.mdx).
- **Four clinical status levels and no more.** `steady`, `watch`, `attention`,
  `urgent` — an ordinal ladder with fixed meanings, fixed tone and a fixed
  budget. See [Clinical status semantics](./clinical-status-semantics.mdx).
- **Status is never carried by one channel alone.** Never colour alone, never
  icon alone, never motion at all. Colour, shape, icon and a word, together,
  every time.
- **Numbers are formatted by rule, not by preference.** Precision, units,
  rounding and the refusal of false precision are a correctness surface, not a
  style choice. See [Numbers, units and precision](./numbers-units-precision.mdx).
- **Uncertainty is rendered, never hidden.** A stale reading, an estimate and a
  measurement are visually distinct. See
  [Uncertainty, staleness and missing data](./uncertainty-and-staleness.mdx).
- **The word "normal" is banned** in any user-facing string that describes a
  person's own result. See [Reference ranges](./reference-ranges.mdx).

What opsinjs never decides, and will refuse to guess at:

- The thresholds. Which value is `watch` and which is `attention` is a clinical
  decision belonging to your product and whoever is clinically accountable for it.
- The reference ranges. They vary by laboratory, assay, age, sex, pregnancy and
  population.
- The words that carry clinical meaning. opsinjs gives you sentence shapes; the
  clinical content of the sentence is yours.
- Whether your product is a medical device. See
  [Regulatory context](./regulatory-context.mdx) and
  [Safety, scope and limitations](../start/safety-scope-and-limitations.mdx).

## Why (evidence)

<ResearchNote evidence="opinion" date="2026-09-02">
  The argument for putting doctrine above components is an engineering one, and
  it is an opinion rather than a finding.

  A design system's real output is not code, it is *consistency of decision*. If
  the decision "which colour means worrying" is taken independently in forty
  screens, it will be taken forty slightly different ways, and the inconsistency
  is invisible in review because each screen looks fine on its own. Encoding the
  decision once — in tokens, in a status vocabulary, in a lint rule — is the only
  mechanism we know of that survives a team growing.

  In a health product the same argument has teeth, because the cost of the
  inconsistency is not aesthetic. A person who has learnt that amber means "look
  at this soon" in one screen will carry that learning into every other screen
  whether or not you honoured it there.

  What would change our mind: evidence that consumers do not in fact generalise
  colour meaning across screens within one app, or that a smaller status
  vocabulary than four levels performs measurably better. We have not run that
  study and we do not cite one.
</ResearchNote>

Where a page in this section carries real evidence it says so in its `evidence`
field and names a source you can open. Where it carries our opinion it says
`opinion` and says which parts we would change our mind about. The full list of
sources is on [Evidence and references](./evidence-and-references.mdx). We have
not, at the time of writing, had this pillar reviewed by an external clinician;
that gap is tracked openly rather than quietly.

## Applying it

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    Read [Clinical interaction guidelines](./clinical-interaction-guidelines.mdx)
    before your first screen, then keep
    [Safety review checklist](./safety-review-checklist.mdx) open during review.
    Ten rules and one checklist cover most of what goes wrong.
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    Treat this section as background reading to come back to. The decisions it
    describes are structural — the colour axes and the status vocabulary are
    expensive to retrofit once thirty screens have made their own choices.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

Three entry points, depending on what you are doing today:

- **Writing a screen that shows a measured value.** Start at
  [Reference ranges](./reference-ranges.mdx), then
  [Numbers, units and precision](./numbers-units-precision.mdx), then
  [Uncertainty, staleness and missing data](./uncertainty-and-staleness.mdx).
- **Writing anything that alerts.** Start at
  [Clinical status semantics](./clinical-status-semantics.mdx), then
  [Alarm fatigue](./alarm-fatigue.mdx), then
  [Emergency and escalation](./emergency-and-escalation.mdx).
- **Writing anything that asks a question.** Start at
  [Consent and disclosure](./consent-and-disclosure.mdx), then
  [Crisis and self-harm](./crisis-and-self-harm.mdx) if any answer could disclose
  risk of harm.

## What this does not cover

This is guidance for **consumer and patient-facing** interfaces: an app someone
uses to look at their own health data, log something about themselves, or be told
something about a result. It is not written for, and should not be used as-is in:

- **Clinician-facing software** — electronic health records, prescribing,
  triage tooling, diagnostic workstations. The reader is trained, the density
  requirements are opposite to ours, and the safety analysis is different.
- **Regulated device user interfaces** — anything that is part of a medical
  device under EU MDR, UK MDR or the FDA's device framework. opsinjs confers no
  regulatory status of any kind.
- **Clinical content** — thresholds, ranges, dosing, triage logic, and any
  sentence whose truth is a clinical claim. Those belong to you and to whoever is
  clinically accountable in your organisation.
- **Legal advice** — [Regulatory context](./regulatory-context.mdx) is a
  starting map, written by engineers and designers, not by lawyers.
