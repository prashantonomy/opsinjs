## What this means

Clinicians are trained, at length, in how to give someone difficult news. The
training covers where to sit, what to say first, how much to say, when to stop
talking, and how to check what the person actually heard. It exists because doing
this badly causes lasting harm, and because doing it well is not intuitive.

An app has none of that. It has a card, a font size, and a moment it did not
choose — 06:40, on a commute, from a notification. It cannot read the room, it
cannot slow down, it cannot answer the question that follows, and it cannot tell
whether the person is alone.

Given that, the honest design position is not "deliver bad news well". It is:
deliver as little as is necessary, as plainly as possible, and put the reader in
control of when and how much more they receive — then make sure there is
somewhere to go.

## The rule

**A result that may be distressing is disclosed deliberately: the reader knows
what is coming before they see it, the language is plain and non-catastrophic,
and the surface always ends in a route to a person.**

**Sequence.**

1. **No ambush.** A result the reader has not asked to see is announced before it
   is shown. "Your results are ready. There is something here worth going through
   — you can look now or later." Then a deliberate action to open it.
2. **The reader chooses the moment.** No auto-open from a notification into a
   full result, no result on a dashboard the reader opened for something else.
3. **Layer the detail.** The plain-language summary first, the numbers behind a
   deliberate step, the clinical detail behind another. Never the reverse.
4. **End with a person, always.** What happens next, who is arranging it, and how
   to reach someone. The last thing on the screen is never the number.

**Language.**

5. **Say the thing.** Vagueness reads as concealment and is more frightening than
   the fact. "Your result shows X" beats "your result requires further
   discussion".
6. **No catastrophising and no false comfort.** Neither "this is very serious"
   nor "don't worry". Both are claims the interface cannot support.
7. **No blame.** Never "you failed to", "your poor control", "you should have".
   Health outcomes are not moral outcomes.
8. **No diagnosis and no prognosis.** The interface reports a result and names
   the next step. It does not say what this means for someone's life.
9. **Short sentences, ordinary words, one idea each.** The reader's comprehension
   is reduced at this moment, and the words are the last thing to be tested; see
   [Voice and tone](../content/voice-and-tone.mdx).
10. **Name what is not known.** "This result on its own does not tell us X" is
    both true and, usually, the reassurance the reader most needs.

**Structure.**

11. **The action is as prominent as the result.** A result with a plan attached
    is a different object from a result alone.
12. **Nothing else competes.** No cross-sell, no rating prompt, no streak, no
    unrelated alert on that screen — the alarm-fatigue suppression rules apply.
13. **It stays available.** People re-read. The result, in the same words, must
    be findable afterwards, and shareable with someone they trust; see
    [Sharing with a clinician](../patterns/sharing-with-a-clinician.mdx).
14. **Offer a way to not see it yet**, where clinically appropriate, and never
    where a delay could cause harm — that case is
    [Emergency and escalation](./emergency-and-escalation.mdx).

## Why (evidence)

<ResearchNote evidence="opinion" date="2026-09-02">
  Opinion, and we are careful about which parts.

  There is a real clinical literature on breaking bad news, and there are
  well-known structured protocols taught in medical education. We do not cite one
  here, because we have not evaluated how well any of them transfers to an
  asynchronous, unattended interface, and citing a clinical communication
  protocol as though it endorsed an app screen would be a misuse of it. If you
  want the underlying material, it is easy to find and worth reading; the
  transfer is the part nobody has settled.

  What we are confident about is the structural asymmetry that drives this page.
  In a consultation the clinician can adapt continuously. An app cannot adapt at
  all, so its only lever is *control*: who decides when the information arrives,
  and how much arrives at once. Rules 1 to 3 spend everything on that lever.

  Rule 5 — say the thing — is in tension with rule 1 and worth naming as a
  tension. Warning someone before showing them a result is itself information,
  and a warning that is too vague creates a period of dread. We resolve it by
  making the warning honest about its own weight ("there is something here worth
  going through") rather than either neutral or ominous. We have not tested that
  phrasing.

  What would change our mind: research on patient preferences for receiving
  results digitally. There is work in this area — the studies cited on
  [Reference ranges](./reference-ranges.mdx) touch it — and a serious review
  would probably sharpen several of these rules.
</ResearchNote>

## Applying it

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    "Your results are ready. One of them is worth going through properly — you
    might want a few quiet minutes." Then a button: "Open results".
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    A push notification reading "Abnormal result — tap to view", which opens
    straight onto a red card. Ambush, diagnosis, and no control.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    "Your result is higher than your laboratory's usual range. Your GP has been
    sent this and will contact you within 5 working days. If you have not heard
    by then, call the surgery."
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    "Your result requires further discussion with your healthcare provider."
    Says nothing, means something, and leaves the reader to imagine what.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    "This result on its own does not tell us why. That is what the next test is
    for." Naming the limit of what is known is the most useful sentence on the
    screen.
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    "Don't worry, this is usually nothing." An unsupported reassurance that will
    be remembered and held against you if it turns out to be something.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    Keep the summary in one place, in the same words, permanently, with a way to
    save or share it.
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    Show it once in a dismissible banner. People re-read difficult news four or
    five times, and they will re-read whatever they can find instead.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

and making the slot optional would mean it is empty in the cases that matter
most.

## What this does not cover

- **What the result means.** All clinical interpretation is yours.
- **Whether a result should be released to the reader at all**, and on what
  delay. That is a clinical and often a regulatory decision in your health
  system, not a design one.
- **Emergency findings**, which follow
  [Emergency and escalation](./emergency-and-escalation.mdx) instead — the
  no-ambush rule does not apply when delay causes harm.
- **Disclosure of risk of harm inside a questionnaire**, which is
  [Crisis and self-harm](./crisis-and-self-harm.mdx).
- **The wording itself in detail**, which is
  [Voice and tone](../content/voice-and-tone.mdx) and
  [Writing status and alerts](../content/writing-status-and-alerts.mdx).
- **Results concerning someone else** — a child, a relative, a person being cared
  for — where consent and tone both change.
