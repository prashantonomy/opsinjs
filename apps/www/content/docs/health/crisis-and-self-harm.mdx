## What this means

Mood questionnaires and wellbeing check-ins are common in consumer health
products. Many of the standard instruments include an item about thoughts of
self-harm or of being better off dead, because clinicians need to know. That item
will, eventually, be answered affirmatively inside your app, by someone alone
with a phone, with no clinician anywhere in the loop.

At that moment the product has done something significant: it has invited a
disclosure it is not equipped to respond to. Whatever happens next is design.

The common failure is a screen that carries on. The score appears, the progress
bar advances, the reader is congratulated for completing the questionnaire, and
the most important thing anyone has said to this product all week is absorbed
into an average. The second failure is the opposite: an alarming interstitial
that reads as a system reacting to a threat, or a phone number thrown at someone
with no acknowledgement of what they just said.

This is the surface where a design system's usual language — components, states,
budgets — is least adequate, and where the right answer most depends on clinical
input your team must actually get.

## The rule

**A disclosure of risk of harm is acknowledged in place, immediately, without
alarm, and always leads somewhere. It is never scored silently, never
congratulated, and never a dead end.**

1. **Acknowledge before anything else.** The next thing the reader sees responds
   to what they said. Not a score, not a progress bar, not a thank-you.
2. **Plain, warm, unshocked language.** "Thank you for telling us. Many people
   have these thoughts, and support is available." No clinical register, no
   alarm, no exclamation marks, no red.
3. **This is not the status axis.** Do not paint a disclosure `urgent` and do not
   dress it as a clinical alert. The visual language of the escalation ladder is
   wrong here; use a calm, prominent, distinct surface.
4. **Always offer a route, always more than one.** Talk to someone now; talk to
   someone later; information to read; a way to tell a clinician already involved
   in their care. People in distress differ enormously in what they can do.
5. **Routes are configured per region and kept current.** opsinjs ships no crisis
   numbers, no services and no opening hours, because a wrong or stale number
   here is worse than none. Your product owns the list, and owns keeping it
   right.
6. **Never block, never trap, never force.** The reader may continue, may leave,
   may say nothing more. An interface that will not let someone move on is an
   interface they will lie to next time.
7. **Never require an account, a payment, or a completed profile** to reach
   support content. Support routes sit outside every wall.
8. **Follow safe-messaging practice.** No method detail, no lethality
   information, no graphic imagery, no framing of suicide as a solution or an
   inevitability. This is established practice in the field, not a stylistic
   preference — see the evidence note.
9. **Be explicit about what happens to the answer.** Whether a person is
   contacted, whether a clinician sees it, and whether anything is triggered.
   Silence here is read as surveillance by some readers and as a safety net by
   others, and both are dangerous guesses.
10. **Design the repeat case.** Someone who discloses every week must not get the
    same interstitial every week, and must not get nothing. This needs clinical
    input, not a product decision.
11. **Do not use engagement mechanics anywhere near this.** No streaks, no
    badges, no "you are on a roll" on a mood questionnaire.

<SafetyCallout severity="attention" evidence="opinion">
  If your product includes any item that can disclose risk of harm, the response
  to that item must be designed with someone who has clinical mental health
  expertise, and it must be written down as a specified pathway rather than
  assembled from screens. If you cannot get that input, seriously consider
  removing the item — asking a question you cannot respond to is its own harm.
</SafetyCallout>

## Why (evidence)

<ResearchNote evidence="opinion" date="2026-09-02">
  We mark this `opinion` because we are describing interface behaviour, and we
  have not run studies on it. But rule 8 rests on a real and well-established
  body of practice that you should go and read directly rather than take from us:
  guidance on responsible communication about suicide has been published by the
  World Health Organization, and in the UK the Samaritans publish media
  guidelines that are widely used well beyond journalism. Both are current
  documents, both are freely available, and both are more authoritative on this
  than a design system can be. We name them and deliberately do not paraphrase
  their content — paraphrasing safe-messaging guidance is exactly the way it gets
  diluted.

  Rules 1 to 4, 6 and 11 are our design judgement, and the judgement rests on one
  asymmetry: a person who has just disclosed something difficult and is met with
  a progress bar learns that this app is not a place to be honest. That lesson
  degrades every future answer, including the ones a clinician needs. We consider
  the cost of an over-gentle response to be near zero and the cost of a
  dismissive one to be high and lasting.

  Rule 3 — keeping this off the clinical status axis — is the one most likely to
  be argued with, because the disclosure is serious and the status axis is where
  serious things go. We keep it separate because the status axis is about values
  against ranges, and because an `urgent` red banner in response to a person's
  disclosure of their own thoughts reads as a machine reacting to a threat.

  What would change our mind: clinical advice. On this page in particular, we
  would defer to it over our own reasoning.
</ResearchNote>

## Applying it

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    "Thank you for telling us — that can be hard to say. If you would like to
    talk to someone now, here is how. You can also carry on, and this will still
    be here."
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    "Question 9 of 9 complete. Your score is 18 — moderately severe." The
    disclosure has been converted into a number and the person into a band.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    Offer several routes with different demands: a helpline, a text service, a
    self-help page, and a way to flag it to a clinician already involved in their
    care. Say which are available now.
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    Show a single phone number and a Close button. Many people in crisis cannot
    make a phone call, and the interface has offered them nothing.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    "Your answers are shared with the clinical team at your surgery. They may
    contact you about this." Say it before the question, and again here.
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    Leave it ambiguous. A reader who suspects an unspecified consequence will
    answer to avoid the consequence, which destroys the value of the question.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    Keep the support surface reachable afterwards — a persistent, quiet entry
    point in the app, not only in the moment of the disclosure.
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    Show it once, on dismissal lose it forever, and require the person to
    re-disclose to find help again.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

## What this does not cover

- **Clinical assessment or risk stratification of any kind.** opsinjs contains no
  instrument, no scoring, no thresholds and no triage.
- **The crisis services themselves** — numbers, hours, coverage, eligibility.
  Yours to configure and to keep current.
- **Safeguarding duties.** Whether you must act on a disclosure, and how, is a
  legal and clinical governance question for your organisation.
- **Content moderation** of anything a user writes freely, which is a different
  discipline again.
- **Children and young people**, where consent, confidentiality and safeguarding
  all differ substantially and this page is not sufficient.
