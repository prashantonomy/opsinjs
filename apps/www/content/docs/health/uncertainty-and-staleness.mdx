## What this means

An interface that shows a number is making an implicit claim: this is the current
value. The claim is usually unspoken, always believed, and frequently false.

The number on the tile might be from a sensor reading taken forty seconds ago. It
might be from a device that last synced on Tuesday. It might be a model's
estimate rather than a measurement. It might be the last successfully fetched
value, rendered because the fetch failed and the cache was still warm. It might
be an average presented in the visual language of a reading. Every one of these
looks identical in most products: a large number in a card.

The consequences are asymmetric in a way that matters. A reader who believes a
stale reassuring value is current will not act when they should. A reader who
believes a stale worrying value is current will act on a problem that has already
resolved. Both are caused by the same missing sentence.

There is also a purely engineering failure that produces the worst version of
this: a nullish value coerced to `0` and rendered in the metric's units. Zero is
a physiologically impossible value for many health metrics and a catastrophic one
for others, and it is what you get for free.

## The rule

**A displayed value carries its recency and its origin. An absence is rendered as
an absence, never as a value.**

**Five data states, visually distinct, defined once for every data surface** —
the full definitions live at
[Data states](../foundations/data-states.mdx) and this page is the health-specific
reading of them:

| State | What it means | What the reader must see |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Fresh** | Measured, current, complete | The value, its unit, when it was taken |
| **Stale** | Real but older than this metric's useful window | The value, visibly de-emphasised, with its age stated and a way to refresh |
| **Partial** | Some sources reported, others did not | The value, plus which sources are missing |
| **Estimated** | Derived or modelled rather than measured | An explicit estimate label, wider rounding, and what it is derived from |
| **Absent** | No data, or a failed fetch | No number at all; a sentence and a next action |

**Rules:**

1. **Every health value has a timestamp available to the reader.** Relative time
   for recency — "20 minutes ago" — with the absolute time available. Relative
   time alone becomes useless past a couple of days.
2. **Staleness is per metric.** A heart rate from four hours ago is stale; a
   cholesterol result from four months ago is current. The threshold is a
   property of the metric and is declared, not assumed.
3. **Stale is a visual state, not a caption.** A reader who does not read the
   caption must still be able to tell. De-emphasise the value itself.
4. **Never show a stale value as if it were fresh, and never hide it instead.**
   Both are worse than showing it and saying how old it is.
5. **An estimate is labelled at the point of the number**, not in a footnote, and
   is rounded more coarsely than a measurement — see
   [Numbers, units and precision](./numbers-units-precision.mdx).
6. **A failure is not an absence and is not a zero.** "We could not load this"
   is a different sentence from "You have not recorded any readings yet", and
   both are different from `0`.
7. **Skeletons never imply a value.** A loading placeholder reserves layout; it
   does not show a plausible number, a shape that looks like a chart, or a
   status colour.
8. **Status degrades with certainty.** A value the app cannot vouch for cannot
   drive an `attention` or `urgent` status. If you are not sure the number is
   current, you are not entitled to alarm someone with it.
9. **Partial is stated, not silently averaged.** "3 of your 4 devices have
   reported today" beats a number that quietly means something different from
   yesterday's.
10. **Offline is a first-class state.** See
    [Offline and stale data](../patterns/offline-and-stale-data.mdx).

## Why (evidence)

<ResearchNote evidence="opinion" date="2026-09-02">
  Opinion. We cite no study and the argument is structural.

  Rule 8 is the one we would defend hardest, and it is the one most often broken,
  usually by accident. An alerting pipeline that runs over cached values will
  cheerfully raise an `attention` banner from a reading that is three days old,
  because the alerting code and the freshness code were written by different
  people at different times. The rule exists to make freshness a precondition of
  escalation rather than a display concern layered on afterwards.

  Rule 3 — that staleness must be visible without reading — follows from the
  audience model on [Who this is for](./who-this-is-for.mdx): a reader who glances
  for three seconds reads the number and nothing else. A caption under a number is
  read by roughly the same people who read a footnote.

  The five-state model itself is borrowed from ordinary data-state design and is
  not health-specific. What is health-specific is the insistence that "estimated"
  is a first-class state rather than a variety of "fresh". Consumer health
  hardware produces a great deal of derived data — a value estimated from an
  optical signal is not the same kind of object as a value from a cuff — and
  collapsing the two is a provenance failure. See
  [Data provenance and device accuracy](./data-provenance-and-device-accuracy.mdx).

  What would change our mind: evidence that visible staleness treatment reduces
  engagement enough to harm outcomes. We would still keep rule 8.
</ResearchNote>

## Applying it

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    "68 bpm · measured 12 minutes ago" — value, provenance and recency in one
    line, with the absolute time on demand.
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    "68 bpm" with a sync timestamp buried in a settings screen. The reader has no
    way to know whether this is now or Tuesday.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    "Last reading 3 days ago" with the value de-emphasised and a refresh action,
    when the metric's freshness window is one day.
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    Keep rendering the three-day-old value at full weight because the tile looks
    empty otherwise. The tile is empty. That is the information.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    "Estimated from your watch. Roughly 7 hours 30 minutes." Label first, coarse
    rounding, no false precision.
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    "7 h 32 m" in the same typography as a measured value. Two minutes of
    apparent precision on a figure the device cannot resolve.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    "We could not load your readings. Check your connection and try again." A
    failure, named, with a next step.
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    Render `0` or `--`. One is a false measurement and the other is a shrug.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

## Components that implement this

{/* Generated from `implements`. Do not restate the list by hand. */}

`RelativeTime` is the enforcement point for rules 1 and 2: it takes a staleness
threshold and switches from relative to absolute rendering past a defined window,
so "3 minutes ago" cannot silently become "just now" for a value from last month.
`Value` refuses to render an unknown as a number at all.

## What this does not cover

- **Freshness windows** for any metric. Clinical, and yours.
- **Caching, sync and conflict resolution**, which are your data layer. This page
  describes what the surface must say about them.
- **The generic data-state definitions**, which are canonical at
  [Data states](../foundations/data-states.mdx).
- **Measurement uncertainty as an interval** — showing a value plus or minus a
  confidence bound. Worth doing where the source supports it and not yet
  specified here.
