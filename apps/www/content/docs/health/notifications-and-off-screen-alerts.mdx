## What this means

Everything else in this section assumes the reader chose to open the app. A push
notification inverts that. It arrives unrequested, on a device that may be on a
kitchen table, face-up, next to someone the reader has not told about their
condition. It is rendered by the operating system in a layout you cannot control,
truncated at a length you cannot predict, possibly read aloud by a car, a watch
or a smart speaker, and it persists on the lock screen until dismissed.

It is also the highest-leverage alarm-fatigue vector you have. A person will
tolerate a cluttered dashboard indefinitely and will disable your notifications
after three bad ones — permanently, at the OS level, including the clinical ones.
The notification permission is spent once.

And in a health product the notification is the surface most likely to disclose
something. "Your test result is ready" is a disclosure. "Your glucose has been
high for three days" is a much larger one.

## The rule

**A notification is a clinical surface with three extra constraints: it discloses
in public, it cannot be recalled, and its permission is spent once.**

**1. Content — write for a lock screen someone else can see.**

- The title says what it is about at the level of a category, never a value:
  "Blood pressure" not "Blood pressure 178/104".
- No result values, no diagnoses, no medication names, and no condition names in
  the visible body. The value lives behind authentication in the app.
- Assume it will be read aloud by a device. Write a sentence that is not
  humiliating when spoken in a room.
- Offer a per-user setting for how much a notification may reveal, defaulting to
  the least. See [On-screen privacy](./on-screen-privacy.mdx).

**2. Escalation — a push is the top of the ladder, not a shortcut to it.**

- The budget from [Alarm fatigue](./alarm-fatigue.mdx) applies: at most one
  non-clinical push per day, and non-clinical pushes never use the clinical
  channel.
- Separate OS notification channels for clinical and non-clinical messages, so a
  user can silence your marketing without silencing your alerts. On platforms
  that support it, this is a hard requirement, not a nicety.
- Never send a `steady` result as a push. "Everything is fine" is not worth a
  spent permission unless the user explicitly asked to be told.

**3. Continuity — the push and the in-app surface are one message.**

- Every push resolves to a specific in-app surface that carries the full message
  at the same status level. Tapping a notification must never land on a
  dashboard.
- The in-app surface is authoritative. If the situation changed between the send
  and the tap, the app says so rather than pretending the push was current.
- A notification is stamped with when the underlying reading was taken, not when
  the message was sent. Those differ, and the difference matters.
- Clearing the notification does not clear the in-app alert. Dismissing a lock
  screen is not acknowledging a clinical message.

**4. Timing — an unrequested interruption has a cost.**

- Respect system quiet hours and platform interruption levels. Reserve the
  highest interruption level for the clinical urgent path, and use it rarely
  enough that it stays meaningful.
- Do not send a health alert at 03:00 unless it is genuinely actionable at 03:00.
  Waking someone to tell them something they can only act on at 09:00 is a net
  harm.
- Never batch a clinical alert behind an engagement digest.

**5. Badges and widgets are notifications too.**

- A badge count on the app icon is visible to anyone who can see the phone, and a
  badge that only ever appears when something is wrong is a disclosure.
- A home-screen widget shows health data to a room. Widgets default to
  category-level information with values hidden until unlocked, where the
  platform allows it.

<SafetyCallout severity="urgent" evidence="opinion">
  A notification must never be the only channel for a message that may require
  emergency care. Notifications fail silently: they are dropped, throttled,
  disabled, or delivered to a device in a drawer. The emergency path is specified
  at [Emergency and escalation](./emergency-and-escalation.mdx) and it is always
  redundant.
</SafetyCallout>

## Why (evidence)

<ResearchNote evidence="opinion" date="2026-09-02">
  This page is opinion. We cite no study, and the reasoning is drawn from
  platform guidance and from the structure of the problem rather than from
  measurement.

  Two things are close to fact and worth stating as such. First, notification
  permissions are revoked at the OS level and are not per-message: a user who
  turns you off has turned off your clinical alerts too. Second, lock screen
  content is visible without authentication by default on the major mobile
  platforms, and users generally do not change that. Both are checkable against
  Apple's Human Interface Guidelines and Android's notification documentation,
  which are the right places to look for current platform behaviour and which we
  cite rather than restate — platform behaviour changes and a copied summary
  would be wrong within a year.

  The clinical-versus-engagement channel split, the one-push-a-day budget, and
  the rule against putting values in a notification body are our judgements. The
  last of these has a real cost — a user who has to unlock the phone to see a
  number will sometimes not bother — and we accept that cost because the failure
  it prevents is worse and irreversible.

  What would change our mind: evidence that users prefer values in notifications
  and understand the disclosure trade-off. Even then we would want it opt-in.
</ResearchNote>

## Applying it

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    "Blood pressure — there is a reading to look at. Open the app to see it."
    Category, an action, no value, no diagnosis, safe to read aloud.
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    "Warning: your blood pressure is 178/104, which is very high." A clinical
    statement, at a value, on a lock screen, to a person who cannot ask a
    question and may not be alone.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    Register two notification channels — "Health alerts" and "Reminders and
    updates" — and label them plainly in settings so the user can keep the first
    and mute the second.
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    Send everything on one channel. The user's only remaining control is to
    silence you entirely, and eventually they will.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    Deep-link straight to the surface the notification is about, and show the
    same status there. One message, two renderings.
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    Open the home screen and hope the reader finds it. You have generated anxiety
    and then withheld the resolution.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    Show when the reading was taken: "Reading from yesterday, 21:40." Delivery
    time and measurement time are different facts.
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    Imply the reading is live. A delayed sync that surfaces a three-day-old value
    as news is both frightening and wrong.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

## What this does not cover

- **Sending notifications.** Scheduling, delivery, tokens, platform APIs and
  reliability are your infrastructure.
- **Email and SMS**, which are worse on every disclosure axis and are outside
  this system.
- **The alarm-fatigue budget itself**, which is
  [Alarm fatigue](./alarm-fatigue.mdx).
- **Watch and wearable surfaces**, which need their own treatment — glanceable,
  frequently public, often haptic-first — and are not yet specified here.
