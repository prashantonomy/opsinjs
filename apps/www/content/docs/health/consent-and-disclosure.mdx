## What this means

Health data is the most sensitive category of personal data there is, and consent
interfaces for it are usually the least designed screens in the product: a wall
of text, a single checkbox, and a button that is the only way forward.

That pattern is a legal artefact rather than a design. It exists to produce a
record that someone agreed, and it is optimised for the record rather than for
the agreement. It reliably produces users who have consented to things they
cannot name, which is bad ethics, weak compliance, and — the part product teams
feel first — a fragile relationship. The moment someone discovers a use they did
not expect, every other permission becomes suspect.

The alternative is not more text. It is asking less, later, in context, and one
purpose at a time.

## The rule

**Consent is per purpose, asked in context, expressed in plain language,
revocable where the data appears, recorded with its version, and re-asked when
the purpose changes.**

1. **Granular, never bundled.** One decision per purpose. "Store my readings",
   "share with my GP", "use my data to improve the app" and "send me health tips"
   are four different questions with four different answers.
2. **Asked in context, not in onboarding.** Ask for location when the feature
   that needs it is used, not on the second screen of setup. Consent given before
   the reader knows what the app does is not informed.
3. **Ask for the minimum, and say so.** Every field collected is a field
   justified. Collecting "for future use" is not a purpose.
4. **Plain language, with the specifics.** Not "to improve your experience" but
   "so your GP can see your blood pressure readings in your record". Name who,
   what and why. See [Plain English A–Z](../content/plain-english-a-z.mdx).
5. **Refusal is a real option and is not punished.** Declining a secondary
   purpose must not block a primary function, and must not be harder than
   accepting. No pre-ticked boxes, no consent bundled into a Continue button, no
   dark patterns in the layout of the two choices.
6. **Revocation lives where the data lives.** A control on the screen showing the
   data, not only in a settings sub-page. Withdrawing is as easy as granting.
7. **Say what happens on withdrawal**, before the reader withdraws: what stops,
   what is deleted, what is kept and why, and what cannot be undone.
8. **Record the version.** Store what was agreed, when, and against which version
   of the wording. A consent record with no version is not evidence of anything.
9. **Re-ask on material change.** A new purpose is a new consent. Silently
   extending an existing one is the failure that destroys trust permanently.
10. **Disclosure is continuous, not a one-time event.** Where data is shared, say
    so at the point it is visible — "your GP can see this" belongs on the screen,
    not in a policy.
11. **Sensitive categories deserve extra care.** Sexual health, mental health,
    reproductive health, substance use, HIV status and genetic data carry
    real-world consequences if disclosed. Ask separately, store separately,
    display with [On-screen privacy](./on-screen-privacy.mdx) in mind.
12. **Consent is not capacity.** An interface cannot assess whether someone
    understands. Design so that understanding is likely, not so that agreement is
    likely.

## Why (evidence)

<ResearchNote evidence="mixed" date="2026-09-02">
  **The legal frame is real and checkable.** In the UK and the EU, data about
  health is a special category under the UK GDPR and the EU GDPR, with additional
  conditions on processing, and consent as a lawful basis has statutory
  requirements: freely given, specific, informed, unambiguous, and as easy to
  withdraw as to give. Those are legislative requirements, not our preferences,
  and rules 1, 4, 5, 6 and 8 restate them in interface terms. Read the
  legislation and your regulator's current guidance rather than this page; we
  cite the frame and deliberately do not summarise the law.

  Consent is also frequently not the right lawful basis for direct care, which is
  a point product teams miss: an interface may need to explain a use without
  asking for consent to it, and asking for consent you will process anyway is
  worse than not asking. This is a question for your data protection lead.

  **Opinion.** Rules 2, 3, 10 and 12 are design judgement. Rule 2 — ask in
  context — trades a tidy onboarding for comprehension, and it costs measurable
  conversion; we think the trade is right and we accept it is a trade. Rule 12 is
  the one we would underline: no consent interface can establish understanding,
  so the goal is to make the wording so ordinary that misunderstanding is
  unlikely, rather than to make the record so thorough that liability shifts.

  What would change our mind: nothing on the legal rules. On rule 2, evidence
  that contextual permission prompts confuse users more than a single upfront
  explanation would make us reconsider the balance.
</ResearchNote>

## Applying it

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    "Share your blood pressure readings with your GP surgery? They will see the
    readings and when you took them. You can stop this at any time from the blood
    pressure screen." Then two equally weighted choices.
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    "I agree to the Terms of Service and Privacy Policy" as a single checkbox
    covering storage, sharing, analytics and marketing. Four decisions collapsed
    into one, none of them informed.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    Put a "Shared with your GP" indicator and a control directly on the data
    screen, so a reader who has forgotten what they agreed to finds out where it
    matters.
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    Bury withdrawal three levels into settings under a different name. A
    revocation that is hard to find is not a revocation that is as easy to
    withdraw as to give.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    "If you stop sharing: your GP keeps the readings they have already seen, and
    no new readings are sent. Your own history in the app is unchanged."
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    "Are you sure? Your care may be affected." A vaguely threatening confirmation
    that trades on fear to reverse a decision the reader is entitled to make.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    Ask for a new consent when the purpose changes, and say what changed: "We
    would now like to use your readings for research. This is new, and it is
    optional."
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    Update the privacy policy and rely on continued use as agreement. Nobody read
    it, everybody knows nobody read it, and the record is worthless.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

## Components that implement this

`ConsentSheet` is specified to require a named purpose, a plain-language
explanation, a stated withdrawal consequence and a version identifier, so a
generic all-purpose consent cannot be constructed from it by leaving fields out.
It presents accept and decline with equal visual weight by construction.

## What this does not cover

- **Legal compliance.** This page is written by designers and engineers. It is
  not legal advice, it is not a compliance checklist, and it does not substitute
  for your data protection lead or your regulator's guidance.
- **Lawful bases other than consent**, and the frequent case where consent is the
  wrong basis for direct care.
- **Records management** — retention, deletion, subject access, portability —
  which are your data layer's obligations.
- **Consent on behalf of another person**: children, people lacking capacity, and
  carers. Substantially different, and not yet specified here.
- **Research consent**, which is a distinct regime with its own governance.
- **The wording of sensitive questions**, which is
  [Asking sensitive questions](../content/asking-sensitive-questions.mdx).
