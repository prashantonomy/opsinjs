## What this means

Privacy work in health products concentrates on the parts that are invisible:
encryption, access control, retention, sharing agreements. All necessary, and all
irrelevant to the most common actual disclosure, which is a person on a train
opening an app while someone sits next to them.

The screen is a publishing surface. Everything on it is visible to whoever can
see the phone, and health apps have design conventions that make the situation
worse rather than better: the most sensitive number is rendered largest, in the
highest contrast, in the position the eye lands on first. A condition name in a
section header can be read from a metre away. An app icon with a badge on a lock
screen tells a story on its own.

There is a second, quieter exposure: the app switcher. When someone backgrounds
an app, the operating system captures a snapshot of the current screen and shows
it in the multitasking view, where it persists and where other people looking at
the device will see it.

## The rule

**Sensitive values are hidden by default where the reader has asked for that, the
app never leaks its content through system surfaces, and re-authentication is
required to reveal.**

1. **A per-user privacy setting exists and is easy to find.** Not buried, not
   opt-out. Default to the more private option for categories the reader has
   marked sensitive.
2. **Hidden means hidden, not obscured.** A blurred number can often be read, and
   a shortened one can be inferred from its length. Replace the value with a
   fixed-width mask, never a distorted rendering of the real thing.
3. **Layout does not shift on reveal.** A masked value occupies the same space as
   the real one, so revealing does not reflow the screen and the presence of a
   value is not itself information.
4. **Hide the label too, where the label is the disclosure.** For sensitive
   categories the section heading is often more revealing than the number.
5. **Blur or replace on backgrounding**, before the system snapshot is taken, so
   the app switcher never holds a readable screen. Restore on return.
6. **Re-authenticate to reveal**, using the platform's biometric or passcode
   flow, with a timeout that is short enough to matter. Always offer a
   non-biometric route: biometrics fail, and not everyone can use them.
7. **Screenshots are a user right, not a threat.** Do not block them — people
   legitimately screenshot results to send to family or a clinician. Do warn once
   when a screenshot of a sensitive screen is taken, and make an intentional,
   redacted share the easier path.
8. **Notifications, badges and widgets are on-screen privacy surfaces**, and are
   governed by
   [Notifications and off-screen alerts](./notifications-and-off-screen-alerts.mdx).
9. **Reveal is per value, per session, and never sticky by default.** A reader
   who revealed one number on a train should not find every number revealed on
   the next launch.
10. **Never require a reveal to use the app.** Navigation, logging and settings
    work with values masked.
11. **Design the shoulder-surfing case in review.** For any screen showing a
    sensitive category, ask what is legible at a metre and at an angle.

## Why (evidence)

<ResearchNote evidence="opinion" date="2026-09-02">
  Opinion. We cite no study and the reasoning is a threat model rather than a
  finding.

  Two behaviours are platform facts rather than opinions, and both are worth
  verifying against current platform documentation rather than trusting a copy in
  a design system: mobile operating systems capture a snapshot of the visible
  screen when an app is backgrounded and display it in the multitasking
  interface, and lock screens display notification content without
  authentication by default. Rule 5 exists because of the first and rule 8
  because of the second.

  Rule 2 — masks rather than blurs — is a small technical point with a real
  consequence. A blur is a lossy transform of the real value and, depending on
  radius and font, is sometimes readable and often guessable, particularly for
  short numeric strings. A mask carries no information. The same applies to any
  reveal-on-hover treatment, which additionally does not exist on touch.

  Rule 7 is the one where we deliberately go against a common instinct. Blocking
  screenshots of health data feels protective and mostly harms the user: sharing
  a result with a family member or a clinician is a legitimate and frequent need,
  and blocking it pushes people to photograph the screen with a second phone,
  which is worse in every respect.

  What would change our mind: evidence that a warning on screenshot changes
  behaviour at all. We suspect it mostly generates annoyance, and we include it
  weakly.
</ResearchNote>

## Applying it

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    Render a masked value as a fixed-width block of the same size and weight as
    the real value, with an explicit reveal control that has an accessible name:
    "Show your result".
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    Apply a CSS blur to the number. It is a rendering of the real value, it is
    often legible at a glance, and it is trivially removed from a screenshot.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    Cover the whole window with an opaque screen on `resign active`, before the
    snapshot, and remove it on return.
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    Leave the results screen live in the app switcher. It is now visible to
    anyone who picks up the phone, for as long as the card persists.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    "Sexual health" collapsed to "Results" with the category name hidden, when
    the reader has marked that category sensitive.
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    Mask the number and leave the heading. The heading was the disclosure.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    Offer "Share a summary" that produces a deliberately composed, redactable
    document.
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    Block screenshots. The reader photographs the screen with another phone, and
    now there is an unencrypted image with no redaction in someone's camera roll.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

## Components that implement this

{/* Generated from `implements`. Do not restate the list by hand. */}

`Value` is specified to support a masked state that reserves identical layout,
because rules 2 and 3 are only reliable if the masking happens at the same place
the formatting does. Surfaces that carry sensitive categories are specified to
take a sensitivity flag rather than each screen inventing its own treatment. Both
of those are still specifications. Nothing that ships at `alpha` today has a
masking, hiding or sensitivity prop of any kind — `MetricTile` says so in its own
file, and the others have no such affordance either — so every rule on this page
is the product's to keep in its own code, and a screen that keeps none of them
will look exactly like a screen that keeps all of them.

## What this does not cover

- **Authentication, session management and device security**, which are your
  application's concern.
- **Encryption at rest or in transit**, and everything else that happens off the
  screen.
- **Screen recording and remote access**, including accessibility services with
  broad permissions on the device.
- **Shared devices and household use**, where the threat model changes
  substantially and account separation matters more than masking.
- **Physical privacy filters**, which are a hardware answer to the same problem.
- **What may appear in a notification**, which is
  [Notifications and off-screen alerts](./notifications-and-off-screen-alerts.mdx).
