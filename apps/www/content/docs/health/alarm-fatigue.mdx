## What this means

Alarm fatigue is the best-documented failure mode in clinical technology. In
hospitals, monitors generate so many alarms that staff — competent, trained,
motivated staff — stop responding to them, silence them pre-emptively, or tune
them out entirely. The alarms are individually reasonable. The aggregate is
noise, and people have been harmed by alarms that fired correctly into a room
where nobody was listening any more.

Consumer health apps reproduce this faithfully and at lower stakes right up until
the day the stakes are not low. A ring closes, a streak breaks, a weekly report
is ready, a device needs charging, a reading is a little high, a subscription is
expiring — and every one of them arrives with the same visual weight and the same
buzz. The user learns, in about a fortnight, that this app's notifications do not
require action. That lesson is what they bring to the alert that did.

The uncomfortable part is that each individual decision to escalate was
defensible. Fatigue is a systemic property. It cannot be prevented by any single
screen's review, which is why it needs a budget rather than a judgement.

## The rule

**Escalation is a finite, explicitly budgeted resource. Every surface that
escalates must be spent against a stated budget, and the budget is enforced at
the screen and at the session, not at the component.**

The budgets opsinjs asks you to hold:

| Scope | Ceiling | Rationale |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One screen | **At most one `urgent` surface**, ever | Two urgent things means the reader must triage, and they are not equipped to |
| One screen | At most two `attention` surfaces | Beyond two, the reader reads none of them |
| One session | At most one interruptive modal that is not user-initiated | An interruption the user did not ask for is the most expensive thing you can spend |
| One day | At most one non-clinical push notification | See [Notifications and off-screen alerts](./notifications-and-off-screen-alerts.mdx) |
| Any surface | `urgent` is reserved for clinical urgency | A product problem is never `urgent`, whatever the growth target |

And the escalation ladder, in order. You may only move up a rung when the rung
below has been used and is insufficient — never because a rung looks more
important:

1. **Ambient** — a `StatusPill` on the value itself. Present, not addressed to
   anyone, costs nothing.
2. **In-place** — a `CareCard` in the flow of the screen, next to the thing it is
   about, with a named action.
3. **Screen-level** — an `AlertBanner` at the top of the screen. Addressed to the
   reader, still not blocking.
4. **Interruptive** — a modal or a sheet the reader must dismiss. Requires that
   the message is time-critical and cannot wait for them to arrive.
5. **Off-screen** — a push notification. The most expensive rung, governed by its
   own page, and never the first attempt.

Three further rules that stop the budget being gamed:

- **No parallel escalation.** The same fact does not get a pill *and* a card
  *and* a banner. One fact, one highest rung, with lower rungs suppressed.
- **De-escalation is as important as escalation.** When a value returns to
  `steady` the surface goes away, and the reader is told once that it did.
- **Repetition is not escalation.** Showing the same `attention` banner on eight
  consecutive opens does not increase the chance of action; it converts an alert
  into furniture. Re-raise on a change of state, not on a timer.

<SafetyCallout severity="urgent" evidence="opinion">
  The one exception to every budget above: a value that may require emergency
  care outranks everything and suppresses all other alerts on the screen. That
  path is specified separately at
  [Emergency and escalation](./emergency-and-escalation.mdx), and the reason it
  is separate is precisely so that it never competes with anything else.
</SafetyCallout>

## Why (evidence)

<ResearchNote evidence="mixed" date="2026-09-02">
  **Cited.** Alarm fatigue in clinical settings is a documented patient-safety
  problem rather than a designer's intuition. The Joint Commission has published
  a Sentinel Event Alert on medical device alarm safety in hospitals (Issue 50),
  and alarm-related hazards have appeared repeatedly in ECRI's annual health
  technology hazards lists. We name these so you can look them up and read the
  current versions; we do not quote them, and we do not claim their findings
  transfer directly to a consumer phone app.

  **The transfer is our opinion.** Hospital alarms and app notifications differ
  in almost every respect except the mechanism we care about: repeated
  low-value signals from a source that is supposed to be trustworthy reduce
  response to high-value signals from that source. We consider that mechanism
  general. We have not measured it in a consumer health product and we cite no
  study that has.

  **The numbers in the budget table are ours and are arbitrary in the sense that
  they were chosen, not derived.** One urgent per screen follows from the
  triage argument. Two attention surfaces per screen is a judgement about
  scanning. One un-requested interruption per session is a judgement about
  goodwill. We publish them as numbers rather than as "use restraint" because a
  number can be reviewed, argued with and enforced, and "use restraint" cannot.

  What would change our mind: any field data at all on consumer health alert
  response rates as a function of alert frequency. We would rather have that than
  our table.
</ResearchNote>

## Applying it

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    Suppress the lower rungs when a higher one fires. If an `AlertBanner` is
    already telling the reader their blood pressure needs checking, the tile
    below shows a `watch` pill, not a second banner.
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    Let every component decide its own urgency independently. Nine independently
    reasonable decisions produce one unreadable screen, and no single code review
    catches it.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    Track alert volume per user as a product metric with a target ceiling, the
    way you track error rate. Fatigue is only visible in aggregate.
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    Measure alerts by click-through alone. A high click-through on a novel alert
    tells you nothing about what the twentieth one will do.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    Tell the reader when something has resolved: "Your blood pressure readings
    are back in your usual range." De-escalation is information, and it rebuilds
    trust in the escalation.
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    Silently remove the banner. The reader is left unsure whether the problem
    went away or the app forgot, and the next banner is trusted less.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    Give engagement, retention and commercial messages their own quiet channel
    that is visually and structurally separate from the clinical one.
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    Borrow the clinical channel for a streak reminder because it gets noticed. It
    gets noticed by spending the credibility of every future clinical alert.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

Sound and haptics have their own budget, and it is smaller than the visual one
because they cannot be ignored politely. See
[Sound and haptics](../foundations/sound-and-haptics.mdx) and
[Motion in health UI](./motion-in-health-ui.mdx).

## Components that implement this

{/* Generated from `implements`. Do not restate the list by hand. */}

`AlertBanner` is specified to expose the budget rather than hide it: a screen
that mounts a second `urgent` banner should fail loudly in development instead of
rendering two. That part is still a specification. The component itself ships at
`alpha` and does warn in development about several things — a missing heading
level, an `attention` or `urgent` banner with no action — but nothing counts
banners across a screen, because a component cannot see its siblings. The budget
is enforced by review today, and this is the behaviour the component will be held
to when something can enforce it.
