## What this means

Unit systems look like a localisation problem. They are not. Localisation
failures are visible: a missing translation shows a key, a wrong date format
looks wrong. A unit failure produces a number that is entirely plausible and
entirely wrong.

Blood glucose is the canonical example. Much of the world reports it in mmol/L,
where everyday values are single digits with one decimal. The United States
reports the same measurement in mg/dL, where everyday values are in the tens and
hundreds. A person who has learnt one scale and is shown the other, unlabelled,
has no way to notice: the number is simply not the number they expected, and the
most natural interpretation of an unexpected number in a health app is that
something has changed about their body.

Body weight has the same problem in a different shape. Someone who thinks in
stones and pounds and is shown `11.3` will read it as eleven stone three pounds.
It is eleven point three stone — eleven stone four — and the difference is a
pound. Small, until it is a paediatric dose calculation.

Temperature is the mild case, because the ranges do not overlap plausibly, and it
is still worth getting right because a fever threshold expressed in the wrong
scale is a wrong threshold.

## The rule

**The unit system is a user preference, stored, respected everywhere, and never
inferred silently. A converted value is labelled as converted, and conversion
happens once, at the display boundary.**

1. **Store canonically, display by preference.** One canonical unit per metric in
   storage and in every API. Conversion is a rendering concern and never round
   trips through storage.
2. **The preference is explicit and per-metric-family where it matters.** People
   genuinely do think in kilograms for weight and feet and inches for height, and
   the system must let them.
3. **Never infer the system from the locale alone.** Locale is a reasonable
   default and a bad assumption: people move, clinicians and patients differ, and
   a device may report in a system the reader does not use.
4. **Never convert silently.** If a reading arrived in one system and is shown in
   another, say so: "Converted from 98 mg/dL."
5. **Ranges, thresholds and statuses convert with the value.** A range in mg/dL
   compared against a value in mmol/L is a silent, catastrophic bug, and it is
   the reason conversion belongs at the boundary rather than sprinkled through
   comparison code.
6. **Round after conversion, using the destination metric's precision** — and
   accept that a round trip is lossy. Never re-derive an original value from a
   rounded conversion.
7. **On input, the unit is part of the field**, always visible, switchable in
   place, and remembered. See
   [Units and numeric entry](../patterns/forms/units-and-numeric-entry.mdx).
8. **Compound imperial values are entered compound.** Stones and pounds is two
   inputs, feet and inches is two inputs. Asking someone for `11.3 st` is asking
   them to do arithmetic in a form.
9. **Plausibility-check on entry against the entered unit**, and question rather
   than reject: "That is much higher than your usual readings — is it in mg/dL?"
10. **Exports, shares and print carry the unit unambiguously**, because the
    recipient may not share the reader's preference. See
    [Sharing with a clinician](../patterns/sharing-with-a-clinician.mdx).

The units that need explicit handling in a consumer health product:

| Measurement | Common systems | The specific hazard |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Blood glucose | mmol/L · mg/dL | Both plausible; ranges do not overlap but neither looks wrong |
| Cholesterol | mmol/L · mg/dL | As above, with more sub-measures to get wrong |
| Body weight | kg · lb · st + lb | Decimal stone misread as stone-and-pounds |
| Height | cm · ft + in | Compound entry; decimal feet is meaningless to most readers |
| Temperature | °C · °F | Fever thresholds are scale-specific |
| Blood pressure | mmHg (universal) | Rarely converted, frequently mis-composed |
| Distance | km · miles | Low stakes, high visibility, still worth consistency |
| Energy | kcal · kJ | Factor of about four; both called "calories" colloquially |

## Why (evidence)

<ResearchNote evidence="opinion" date="2026-09-02">
  This page is opinion. We cite no study, and the argument does not need one: the
  failure mode is arithmetic, and the reason it is dangerous is that it is
  undetectable by the reader.

  The one claim worth stating carefully is the design decision to treat units as
  a **correctness** surface rather than a localisation surface, and to ship them
  now rather than behind an internationalisation layer. The reasoning is
  recorded in the project's decision log: translation can be retrofitted onto
  strings, but a comparison between a value in one unit and a threshold in
  another is a defect that a translation layer would never surface. See
  [No lang segment yet](../project/decisions/0005-no-lang-segment-yet.mdx).

  We also state plainly that we do not ship conversion factors as clinical
  content. Conversions between molar and mass concentration depend on the
  molecular weight of the substance being measured, which means the factor is a
  property of the analyte, not of the units. Publishing a table of factors here
  would invite someone to apply the glucose factor to cholesterol. Your clinical
  source owns the factor; the interface owns the labelling.

  What would change our mind: nothing about the rules. The open question is where
  the preference should live — per user, per metric family, or per device — and
  we have chosen per metric family on judgement alone.
</ResearchNote>

## Applying it

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    "5.4 mmol/L" with a visible, tappable unit control, and the reader's choice
    remembered across the whole product.
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    "5.4" with the unit in a legend at the bottom of the screen, or only in the
    chart axis. The value and its unit travel together or not at all.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    "11 st 4 lb" as two labelled inputs, stored as kilograms, displayed back in
    the same form it was entered.
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    "11.3 st" in a single decimal field. Almost every reader will enter or read
    it as stones and pounds, and the app will be a pound out without complaint.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    "This reading came from your meter in mg/dL. Shown here as 5.4 mmol/L."
    Provenance and conversion stated together.
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    Convert on import and store the converted value. The original is now
    unrecoverable, and every later precision decision inherits the rounding.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    Default from locale, then ask once, plainly, in onboarding: "Which units do
    you use for weight?"
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    Infer from the device region and never ask. A UK phone does not mean a
    kilogram reader, and the app will be silently wrong for the whole
    relationship.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

remember.

## What this does not cover

- **Conversion factors and clinical correctness.** opsinjs ships no factors and
  no thresholds. The analyte determines the factor; your clinical source owns it.
- **Translation of the surrounding language**, which is
  [Internationalisation](../handbook/internationalisation.mdx).
- **Number formatting itself**, which is canonical at
  [Numbers, units and precision](./numbers-units-precision.mdx).
- **Dose calculation of any kind.** If your product calculates a dose, unit
  handling is a safety-critical function and this page is not sufficient.
- **Non-SI clinical units** that are neither metric nor imperial — international
  units, and unit systems specific to a single assay. Handle them as their own
  metric with a fixed unit.
