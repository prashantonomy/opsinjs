## What this means

"This raises your risk by 50%."

That sentence is the most misleading construction in health communication, and it
is almost always literally true. It is a relative risk: it describes the ratio
between two probabilities and says nothing at all about their size. If the
underlying risk was 2 in 10,000, the sentence describes a change to 3 in 10,000 —
one additional person in ten thousand. If the underlying risk was 40 in 100, it
describes something entirely different. The reader cannot tell which, and the
number that sounds most alarming is the one that carries the least information.

Percentages have a second problem specific to a lay reader. A percentage is a
normalised ratio, and normalisation is the operation people find hardest. "10%
of people" is understood far less reliably than "1 in every 10 people", and
comparisons between percentages with different denominators — a 10% chance of one
thing against a 5% chance of another that is ten times worse — collapse quickly.

Health interfaces show probabilities constantly: risk scores, likelihoods,
confidence figures, "people like you" comparisons. Almost all of them are shown
in the format that reads worst.

## The rule

**Express risk as an absolute frequency, with a stated population, over a stated
time window, alongside the baseline it is being compared to.**

1. **Absolute, never relative alone.** If a relative figure is given at all, the
   absolute figures on both sides are given with it and are more prominent.
2. **Natural frequencies over percentages.** "About 3 in every 100 people" rather
   than "3%". Keep the denominator constant across every figure in a comparison —
   changing denominators is where lay comparison fails.
3. **Always state the population.** "3 in every 100 people of your age with a
   similar reading" is a different claim from "3 in every 100 people", and the
   reader is entitled to know which one is being made about them.
4. **Always state the time window.** A risk with no window is not a number. "In
   the next 10 years" is part of the figure, not context around it.
5. **Give the baseline.** A risk figure alone is uninterpretable; the reader
   needs the comparison the number is implicitly making.
6. **Never present both sides of a frequency as if only one existed.** If 3 in
   100 experience something, 97 in 100 do not, and saying so is not spin — it is
   the other half of the same fact.
7. **Never use a bare percentage as a headline.** Not in a tile, not in a
   notification, not as a dial value with no explanation.
8. **Do not visualise small probabilities as areas or angles.** A pie slice at 3%
   is unreadable and an arc at 3% looks like an error. Use a countable
   representation — an array of 100 marks — or use words.
9. **Never imply personal certainty from a population figure.** A risk score is a
   statement about a group the reader resembles, not a prediction about the
   reader.
10. **A model's confidence is not the reader's risk.** If a figure is a model
    output, say what the model is and how well it is known to perform. See
    [Data provenance and device accuracy](./data-provenance-and-device-accuracy.mdx).

## Why (evidence)

<ResearchNote evidence="opinion" date="2026-09-02">
  We mark this page `opinion`, and the reason is worth being precise about.

  The substance of rules 1, 2 and 6 is not original to us. There is a substantial
  and long-established literature on risk communication — most closely associated
  with work on natural frequencies and on the distinction between absolute and
  relative risk, and reflected in the editorial policies of several major medical
  journals, which require absolute figures alongside relative ones. Guidance
  bodies producing patient-facing material have followed the same line for years.

  We name the school of thought rather than a specific paper deliberately. This
  documentation's rule is that a citation must be a source we have read and can
  point you at, not a reference reconstructed from memory, and a fabricated or
  mis-attributed citation on a page about honest numbers would be an unusually
  poor joke. If you need the primary sources, search for "natural frequencies
  risk communication" and "absolute versus relative risk reporting" and you will
  find the canonical material immediately.

  Rules 3, 4, 5, 7 and 8 are our design judgement about how the literature's
  conclusions should constrain a component. Rule 8 in particular is a graphical
  claim we have not tested: small angular and areal encodings are known to be
  read poorly in general, and we extend that to risk visualisation on judgement.

  What would change our mind: nothing about absolute-over-relative. Rule 2's
  preference for frequencies over percentages is worth revisiting for numerate
  readers, and a product that knows its audience might reasonably offer both.
</ResearchNote>

## Applying it

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    "About 4 in every 100 people with a similar reading have this happen in the
    next 10 years. For people with a reading in the usual range it is about 2 in
    every 100."
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    "Your risk is doubled." True, uninterpretable, and frightening in proportion
    to nothing.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    "About 3 in every 100 — which means about 97 in every 100 do not." Both
    halves, same denominator.
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    "3% chance" as a headline number on a tile, with the explanation behind a
    disclosure triangle nobody opens.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    Show a grid of 100 marks with 3 highlighted, with a text summary that says
    the same thing for anyone who cannot see the grid.
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    Show a donut chart with a 3% arc. It reads as a rendering bug, and it fails
    entirely for a screen reader without the text twin.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    "This score is based on readings from people of a similar age and sex. It
    describes a group, not you."
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    "Your heart age is 62." A single fabricated-feeling number, personalised in
    grammar and statistical in substance, with no window and no population.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

`ScoreDial` is the component most at risk of violating this page, because a dial
is an inherently confident-looking object. Its specification therefore requires
that the band is named in words, that the derivation is disclosed on the same
surface, and that no dial may render a bare percentage as its primary value.

## What this does not cover

- **Calculating risk.** opsinjs ships no risk models and no scores. If your
  product computes one, its validity, its population and its performance are
  yours to establish and to disclose.
- **Whether a risk figure should be shown at all**, which is a clinical and
  ethical decision — see [Delivering difficult results](./delivering-difficult-results.mdx).
- **Genetic and hereditary risk**, which carries family-disclosure implications
  well beyond this system.
- **Screening statistics** — sensitivity, specificity and the positive predictive
  value problem — which deserve their own page and do not have one yet.
- **Chart construction**, which is
  [Data visualisation](../foundations/data-visualisation/index.mdx).
