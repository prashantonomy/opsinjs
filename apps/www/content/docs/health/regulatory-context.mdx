## What this means

Teams building consumer health software usually discover regulation in one of two
ways. Either someone asks early, gets a frightening answer, and the product is
scoped conservatively; or nobody asks, the product ships, and the question
arrives later attached to a customer, an investor or a regulator.

The uncomfortable truth is that the boundary is genuinely blurred. A step counter
is not a medical device. Software that takes a person's readings and tells them
what to do about them may well be one, and the line between "displaying
information" and "supporting a decision" is exactly where consumer health
products live. Several of the design decisions in this documentation — no
diagnosis, no prognosis, thresholds owned by you, provenance stated — are
positioned deliberately with respect to that line.

What a design system can do here is small and worth being precise about. opsinjs
can make certain evidence easier to produce: a documented, versioned
justification for interface decisions, measured accessibility conformance, and a
clear statement of which decisions the system took and which it left to you. It
cannot make your product compliant with anything.

## The rule

**Establish which regimes apply before you design the surfaces that might trigger
them, and treat opsinjs as documentation input, never as a compliance claim.**

### The regimes to ask about

**NHS clinical risk management standards (England).** Two information standards
govern the clinical risk management of health IT. **DCB0129** applies to the
manufacture of health IT systems; **DCB0160** applies to their deployment and
use by a health organisation. In practice they require a named clinical safety
officer, a hazard log, a clinical safety case and evidence that identified
hazards have been mitigated. If you intend to sell into the NHS, this is usually
the first conversation, and it arrives earlier than you expect. Read the current
published standards; do not rely on a summary, including this one.

**EU and UK medical device regulation.** Under the EU Medical Device Regulation
(Regulation (EU) 2017/745), software can be a medical device in its own right,
and the classification rule for software — Rule 11 in the Regulation's
classification annex — pushes a great deal of decision-supporting software above
the lowest risk class. The UK has its own regime and its own route to market, and
the two have diverged. The question to ask is not "are we a medical device" in
the abstract but "what is our intended purpose, as stated in our own marketing
and interface copy" — because intended purpose is what determines the answer, and
your product copy is evidence of it.

**United States.** Software as a Medical Device is the international framing;
domestically the FDA's approach to clinical decision support software, and the
statutory exclusions for certain software functions introduced by the 21st
Century Cures Act, are where consumer health products usually sit or try to sit.
The distinction between software that informs a person and software that directs
a decision matters a great deal, and the current guidance documents are the place
to establish where you fall.

**Data protection.** Health data is a special category under the UK and EU GDPR
with additional conditions on processing; other jurisdictions have their own
regimes, and in the United States the applicability of HIPAA depends on who you
are rather than on what the data is — a consumer app is frequently outside it,
which surprises people in both directions. See
[Consent and disclosure](./consent-and-disclosure.mdx).

**Accessibility law.** In the EU, the European Accessibility Act brings certain
consumer-facing digital products into scope; in the UK, public sector bodies have
accessibility regulations and the Equality Act applies more broadly; in the US,
the ADA has been applied to digital services and Section 508 governs federal
procurement. opsinjs contributes real evidence here — see
[Conformance report](../accessibility/conformance-report.mdx) — but the
obligation is on your product.

### What opsinjs contributes, precisely

- **Design decisions with recorded rationale and dates**, which is a common gap
  in a clinical safety case: hazard mitigations that rest on interface behaviour
  need a citable justification for that behaviour.
- **Measured accessibility conformance** for the system's own tokens and, when
  components exist, for the components — dated, reproducible and published.
- **An explicit statement of the boundary**: thresholds, ranges, clinical wording
  and interpretation are yours, stated on every page that touches them.
- **A vocabulary for hazards.** The prohibitions on
  [Clinical interaction guidelines](./clinical-interaction-guidelines.mdx) map
  reasonably well onto interface hazards in a hazard log.

### What opsinjs does not do

- Confer, imply or support any regulatory status.
- Constitute a clinical safety case, a hazard log, or evidence of clinical risk
  management under any standard.
- Make your product a non-device, or keep it one.
- Provide legal, regulatory or clinical advice.

## Why (evidence)

<ResearchNote evidence="mixed" date="2026-09-02">
  **Cited by name, not summarised.** DCB0129, DCB0160, Regulation (EU) 2017/745,
  the UK medical devices regime, the FDA's clinical decision support guidance and
  the 21st Century Cures Act software provisions are all real, published,
  findable documents. We name them so you know what to search for and we
  deliberately do not paraphrase their requirements. Regulatory text changes,
  paraphrases decay, and a decayed paraphrase in a design system is worse than a
  blank page because someone will rely on it.

  **Opinion.** The claim that a design system can contribute usefully to a
  clinical safety case is ours, and it is modest: documented, dated design
  rationale is an input, not a mitigation. We have not had that claim tested by a
  clinical safety officer against a real hazard log, and until we have we will not
  put it more strongly.

  We also state an opinion about sequencing that teams often get wrong: the
  regulatory question should be asked before the product's copy is written,
  because intended purpose is established partly by what you say your product
  does. It is easier to design within a boundary than to rewrite an interface
  that has already claimed to interpret results.

  What would change our mind: a clinical safety officer's assessment of what is
  actually useful to them from an upstream design system. We would rewrite this
  section around it.
</ResearchNote>

## Applying it

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    Write your intended purpose down in one paragraph, early, and check every
    piece of interface copy against it. "Helps you record and view your readings"
    and "tells you what your readings mean" are different products.
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    Let marketing copy make a claim the product is not built or regulated to
    make. Intended purpose is established by what you say, and your own screens
    are the evidence.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    Keep a hazard log from the first sprint, and add an entry each time a screen
    could mislead someone. Interface hazards are the cheapest to mitigate early.
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    Start clinical risk management when the NHS procurement questionnaire
    arrives. Retrofitting a safety case onto a finished product is a rebuild in
    disguise.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    Cite opsinjs as design rationale where it helps — "the alert escalation model
    follows a published, dated specification" — with a link and a version.
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    Present opsinjs as evidence of compliance. It is not, it says so, and a
    reviewer will read the page.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

## What this does not cover

- **Everything.** This is a map of where to look, not a survey of the field, and
  it names only the regimes we most often see consumer health teams encounter.
- **Jurisdictions beyond the UK, EU and US**, which have their own regimes.
- **Quality management systems**, clinical evaluation, post-market surveillance
  and vigilance — the substantial machinery that follows if you are a device.
- **Clinical governance** inside a health organisation, which is separate again
  from device regulation.
- **Reimbursement, procurement and assurance frameworks**, which frequently ask
  harder practical questions than the regulator does.
- **Advice.** Get advice.
