## What this means

A reference range is a statistical statement about a population, usually
constructed so that a defined majority of an apparently healthy reference group
falls inside it. It is produced by a specific laboratory, using a specific assay,
for a specific population, and it varies between laboratories for the same test.

Read that again and notice what it does not say. It does not say that a value
inside the range is healthy. It does not say that a value outside it is disease.
By construction, a proportion of perfectly well people fall outside — that is
what "a defined majority" means — and a person can be seriously unwell with every
value comfortably inside.

Now consider what the interface does with this. It prints the range as a bar, the
value as a dot, and labels the middle "Normal". The reader — reasonably —
concludes that they are normal, or that they are not. There is no gentler word in
English for the negation of "normal" applied to a person, and there is no
statistical caveat that survives contact with it.

This is the single most common way a well-built health interface misleads
someone, and it is a wording and layout problem, which means it is ours.

## The rule

**Show the value, show the range it is being compared against, name whose range
it is, and never characterise the person.**

**Banned words in any user-facing string about a person's own result:**

| Banned | Because | Use instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| normal / abnormal | Describes the person, not the measurement; its negation is unbearable | "in the usual range" / "outside the usual range" |
| healthy / unhealthy | A verdict on a life, from one number | "in the usual range" / "higher than the usual range" |
| good / bad | Moral framing of something largely outside the reader's control | Say the direction: "higher", "lower" |
| poor / failed | Failure language for a body | "lower than expected" |
| perfect / optimal | Sets up every future reading as a decline | "in the usual range" |
| positive / negative | Inverted in lay usage — a "positive" result is usually the bad one | Name the finding: "X was found" / "X was not found" |
| elevated | Clinical register; means little to a lay reader | "higher than" |

**Presentation rules:**

1. **The range is always attributed.** "Your laboratory's usual range" or "the
   range this device uses" — never a floating, unowned band.
2. **The range is shown, not merely applied.** If the value is compared to a
   range, the reader can see the range. A status pill without a visible range
   asks the reader to trust an invisible rule.
3. **The boundary is not a cliff.** A value just outside a range is
   presentationally close to one just inside. Do not let a one-unit change
   flip the whole surface from calm to alarming with no gradient in between.
4. **Direction is stated in words**, not left to the reader to infer from the
   position of a dot: "higher than the usual range".
5. **Nothing on the surface asserts a cause.** "Higher than the usual range" is
   an observation. "You may be dehydrated" is a diagnosis.
6. **A range that does not apply is not shown.** If your range is derived from an
   adult population and the reader is fifteen, show no range rather than the
   wrong one.
7. **The reader can always see the raw value**, whatever else the surface does
   with it. A gauge is an aid; the number is the fact.

## Why (evidence)

<ResearchNote evidence="mixed" date="2026-09-02">
  **Cited.** How laboratory results are presented to patients has been studied
  directly. The 2024 systematic review of laboratory-result presentation formats
  in JMIR, [10.2196/53993](https://doi.org/10.2196/53993), examines the formats
  used to communicate results to patients and their effects on comprehension. The
  2018 study in BMC Medical Informatics and Decision Making,
  [10.1186/s12911-018-0589-7](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12911-018-0589-7),
  examines patient-portal presentation of results specifically. We cite both as
  the anchors for the claim that presentation format materially changes what a
  lay reader takes away from a number, and that a bare value with a reference
  interval — the format a portal inherits from a laboratory report — is a poor
  format for a lay reader.

  We deliberately do not extract a specific effect size or a specific
  recommendation from either paper into this page. Read them; they are short.

  **Opinion.** The banned-word list is ours. "Normal" is the one we hold most
  firmly, and the argument is linguistic rather than empirical: the clinical
  sense of "normal" (within a reference interval) and the lay sense (unremarkable,
  acceptable, not wrong with you) are different words that happen to be spelled
  the same, and the interface has no way to signal which one it means. Its
  antonym is the problem. There is no version of being told you are "abnormal"
  that lands lightly.

  Rule 3 — no cliff at the boundary — is also opinion, and it has a real tension
  with clarity: a clear boundary is easier to read. We accept slightly less
  clarity to avoid the failure where a value moving by less than the measurement
  error changes the emotional content of the whole screen.

  What would change our mind: evidence that lay readers correctly interpret
  "normal" in its statistical sense in context. We would be surprised.
</ResearchNote>

## Applying it

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    "5.4 mmol/L — in your laboratory's usual range (3.9 to 5.6 mmol/L)." The
    value, the comparison, the owner of the range, the range itself.
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    "5.4 — Normal ✓". A tick and the word "normal" together read as a clean bill
    of health, which one measurement cannot give.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    "Slightly above your laboratory's usual range. This often needs no action on
    its own — your clinician will look at it alongside your other results."
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    "ABNORMAL — out of range." Shouting an unexplained clinical adjective at
    someone who cannot ask a follow-up question until Monday.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    Render the range as a labelled band with its endpoints visible, and put the
    value inside it. A reader can then judge "just outside" versus "far outside"
    for themselves.
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    Render a red-to-green gradient with no numbers. A gradient asserts a
    continuous quality scale that reference ranges do not have, and it fails
    entirely in greyscale.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    Where sex-, age- or pregnancy-specific ranges exist, use the one that applies
    and say which: "usual range for adults over 18".
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    Apply one adult range to everyone because it is the one in the config file.
    A wrong range is worse than no range: it manufactures a false status.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

## Components that implement this

`RangeBar` is specified to require an attributed range — a bare pair of numbers
with no owner is a validation error rather than a rendered bar — and the
banned-word list above is enforceable as a lint rule over user-facing strings,
which is where it belongs rather than in a reviewer's memory.

## What this does not cover

- **The ranges themselves.** opsinjs ships no reference ranges for any metric.
  They come from your laboratory, your device manufacturer, or your clinical
  team, and they come with an owner.
- **Deciding a status from a range.** Being outside a range is not automatically
  `attention`; see [Clinical status semantics](./clinical-status-semantics.mdx).
- **Number formatting**, which is canonical at
  [Numbers, units and precision](./numbers-units-precision.mdx).
- **Risk expressed as a probability** rather than a range, which is
  [Risk and statistics](./risk-and-statistics.mdx).
- **Composite scores** with no reference interval at all, where the band names
  are invented by the product and must be disclosed as such.
- **The wider vocabulary.** Full plain-English replacements live at
  [Plain English A–Z](../content/plain-english-a-z.mdx).
