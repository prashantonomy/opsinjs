CareCard is where a health product stops describing and starts asking. That makes
attribution the whole specification: the reader must always be able to tell whether a
clinician said this, a rule fired, or the product is guessing. It is also the only card in
the system that carries two different vocabularies about time and urgency at once, and the
decision that shapes everything below is that it maps neither onto the other.

## Preview

The three timings, one under the other, as a controlled comparison rather than a feature
tour: the three cards carry the same instruction, the same author, the same reason and the
same action, and the only thing that differs is the timing sentence. All three timings
render at the same type step, in the same ink, in the same position, with no glyph and no
coloured rule. That is what makes the claim that urgency lives in words checkable rather
than something you have to take on trust. Open it in greyscale and nothing is lost, because
there was never any colour carrying the timing to begin with. The demo ships no deadline:
`dueBy` and `urgency` are independent inputs, and a fixed calendar date beside a relative
phrase is a pairing no demo should teach.

A CareCard with no verb is not a CareCard. If you cannot write the instruction as *do this
thing, by this time*, what you have is information, and information belongs in a
[Callout](./callout.mdx) where it is not dressed up as a demand.

## Clinical meaning

**What it asserts.** That someone the card names is asking this reader to do this specific
thing, optionally by a stated date, in response to a stated reason. Four claims, and the
author is one of them.

**What it must never be read as.** Advice from a clinician who has assessed this person,
unless a clinician has assessed this person and the card says so. This is the most important
sentence on the page. A card that says *repeat this test in three months* with no author is
read as clinical advice, and the product has no way to withdraw a claim it never explicitly
made. So `attribution` is required, and when it arrives empty the card says in words that it
does not know who is asking rather than dropping the line. That is an admission the reader
can act on, and a development warning the author cannot miss.

**Two vocabularies, and no mapping between them.** `urgency` says *when the reader should do
the thing this card is asking of them*. `status` says *how much attention the reading that
prompted the card needs*. Those are different questions with different owners. A clinician
can call a reading `attention` and still ask for a repeat whenever the reader next has a
moment; an app with no clinical assessment at all can ask somebody to do something today.
The card therefore ships no table from one to the other, derives neither from the other in
either direction, and refuses no combination: all fifteen pairs render, and each of them is
the product's to justify. A mapping would let a product set one and silently get the other,
which is the failure this separation exists to prevent.

**The deadline is not derived from the timing either.** `dueBy` is a calendar date the
product supplies; the timing phrase is a relative one the component writes; nothing compares
them. A card can therefore say *do this within a week* above a date five weeks out, and it
renders. Refusing the pair would mean opsinjs deciding which of the two a reader should
obey, and opsinjs has no basis for that decision any more than it has for mapping `urgency`
onto `status`. Keeping the two coherent is the product's work, and the reliable way to do it
is to set one of them rather than both: the phrase when the deadline is soft, the date when
it is real.

**Is a steady CareCard a contradiction?** No. This page has been asking that since it was a
stub, so here is the answer in full. `steady` describes a reading that is where it was
expected to be. It does not describe an empty diary. *Book your next check when you next get
a chance, because everything we have seen is where we expected it* is the commonest care
instruction there is, and the one products forget to build because they reach for a card
only when something has gone wrong. Refusing it would push products into inventing a fourth
urgency for it, which is how a three-value vocabulary quietly becomes a five-value one.

**Which colour axis it may use.** The **status** axis, in exactly one place: an embedded
[StatusPill](./status-pill.mdx), which carries the word, the glyph, the colour and
`data-status` together. Nothing else on the card takes a status colour. The surface does not,
the boundary does not, and the heading rule does not. The **category** axis may not appear at
all: a card telling somebody to contact their clinic is not a sleep card or a heart card, it
is an instruction, and tinting it by category makes the instruction look like a topic.

This is a deliberate departure from the specification this page carried at `planned`, which
put status on the card's heading rule and icon. A rule tinted `urgent` sitting above the
words *when you next get a chance* gives the reader a colour to read the timing from, and
the two vocabularies do not map. The colour and the words would therefore be free to
disagree, with the colour winning. Every departure of this kind is recorded rather than
quietly taken; this one is the largest on the page.

**Urgency lives in the heading.** *Do this today*, *do this within a week* and *when you
next get a chance* are phrases inside the heading element, supplied by the component from a
fixed table of three, so the claim holds whatever copy the product writes. A reader who
scans only the headings comes away with the right timing, and a reader with no colour
perception loses nothing.

**The card does not know what time it is.** The timing words are relative and the card reads
no clock: it holds no timer, polls nothing, and re-renders on nothing. A card rendered at
half past eleven at night still says *Do this today* at ten past midnight, and now means a
different day. Nothing here detects that, and nothing here should. A component that
escalated its own urgency on a timer would be raising the temperature of somebody's health
screen on a schedule nobody agreed to. The parts built to survive midnight are the absolute
ones: `dueBy` renders as a written calendar date, because a date does not drift, and
`overdue` is a boolean the product supplies rather than a comparison the card performs.
Working out whether a date is behind somebody needs that person's own time zone, which a
component rendered on a server does not have. A product that keeps a screen alive across a
day boundary owns re-rendering it.

**Who owns the rules.** The consuming product. CareCard has no clinical logic, no default
timings, no library of instructions, and no emergency number.

<SafetyCallout severity="urgent" evidence="opinion">
At most one urgent surface per screen, and the axis that budget is counted on is the status
axis, not the timing one: several cards may honestly say *do this today* on the same
morning, and only one thing on a screen may be `urgent`. An urgent card names an action the
reader can actually take right now. A card that says *seek medical attention immediately*
with no number, no link and no next step has transferred the product's anxiety to the reader
without transferring anything useful. If the instruction is to ring emergency services, say
the words and give the number for the reader's country. The component ships neither, and
renders a `tel:` action exactly as it renders any other link.
</SafetyCallout>

**The one safety-checklist item this component cannot answer.** Item F3 of the
[safety review checklist](../health/safety-review-checklist.mdx) asks whether the emergency
path is implemented in full and has been tested this release. For CareCard the answer is no,
and it will stay no: the component ships no number, no country, no opening hours and no
emergency mode, and it renders a `tel:` action exactly as it renders any other link. That is
recorded here as an accepted exception rather than as an open question, because a component
cannot answer F3 at all. The item is scoped to a screen, and the emergency path belongs to
the product that assembles one. The owner is the consuming product's clinical owner and the
reason is that opsinjs does not know the reader's country. No reviewer name and no date are
recorded against this page, because no clinical review of this component has happened;
`a11yDate` is `null` for the same reason, and neither will be filled in until one does. See
[Emergency and escalation](../health/emergency-and-escalation.mdx).

The parts above are identified by `data-slot` and are styleable from outside. None of them
is an export: there is no `CareCard.Heading` component, because every one of them takes its
content from a prop and a compound export would be a second way to build a card that could
disagree with the first. `Card` is the exception in the tree rather than in the API. It is
a real composed component, and its own material, padding and print boundary are what the
CareCard sits in.

A CareCard without an author is a composition error. It does not throw and it does not
render empty: it renders the instruction, states in words that nobody has been named, and
warns once in development. Everything else on the list is optional; the author is not.

## Examples

### A steady card that still asks for something

The answer to this page's oldest open question, as code. `status="steady"` and
`urgency="when-convenient"` on one card is not a contradiction. It is the routine follow-up
that products forget to build. Neither prop is derived from the other, and the card would
render the same way if the two disagreed harder than this.

<ComponentPreview name="care-card-steady-and-still-asking" kind="example" align="start" />

### A deadline the reader is already behind

`dueBy` is a calendar date and renders as one. `overdue` is a boolean the product supplies,
because working out whether a date has gone by needs the reader's own time zone. The card
says so in words and does nothing else. There is no colour change, no warning glyph and no
escalation on a timer. What to do about it is the product's to say, and it says it by
changing the heading.

<ComponentPreview name="care-card-a-deadline-that-has-passed" kind="example" align="start" />

### The author is missing

The same instruction twice, with and without an author, so the difference is visible rather
than argued. The unattributed card is what a reader fills in with the most authoritative
answer available; the admission is deliberately unlovely, because an author who sees it
fixes it. Because the instruction is the control variable, both cards carry the same
heading. The heading is the card's accessible name, so the two regions are named identically
and a caption above each is what separates them for a reader going through in order. A
reader picking a region out of a list of landmarks still hears the same name twice, and
nothing this example can do would change that.

## Content guidelines

Start with a verb, and let the card supply the timing. The three phrases are the component's
so that the claim holds on every card in a product; writing *in the next week* into the
heading as well says the same thing twice and invites the two to disagree after a rewrite.

Attribute in every card, in the reader's words: *your GP surgery asks*, *your clinic's
automatic reminder*, *this app, based on the range you set*. Say the reason in one sentence.
A reader who understands why will comply more reliably and worry less than one who has only
been told.

Never write *you must*, *you need to*, *failure to*, or *it is important that you*. They add
pressure without adding information, and pressure is the thing this component is most likely
to over-supply.

Action labels say what activating them does. *Book a repeat test* is an action; *learn more*
is a link pretending to be one. A telephone action carries the number in its own label, so
that it is visible as text as well as reachable as a `tel:` link, and so that a reader who
cannot dial from this device can still read it out.

A reason that compares a reading to a range assumes the range is on screen, and this card
does not show one. It renders neither the value nor the band it was measured against, so
*your last reading was outside the range* asks the reader to take an invisible rule on
trust. That is what [reference ranges](../health/reference-ranges.mdx) rules out. Either
put the comparison on the [ResultCard](./result-card.mdx) or [RangeBar](./range-bar.mdx)
beside the card, where the range is visible, and let the reason say what is being asked for;
or write a reason that does not invoke a comparison at all. The examples on this page take
the second route.

## Accessibility

Some of these are checked by a gate on every commit. The rest are arguments, and they are
labelled as such: `alpha` promises that accessibility is claimed only where it has been
checked, with the gaps listed rather than left out.

**Checked, by `pnpm run check:a11y` on every commit:**

- **No status colour anywhere on the card.** The static rig fails the build on any file that
  paints a status colour or stamps `data-status` without also reading `CLINICAL_STATUS_META`
  and a glyph. This file does none of the three, because the whole status axis on a CareCard
  is the [StatusPill](./status-pill.mdx) it composes. That pill is checked in turn, on its
  own file. Said exactly: the rule does not fire here, which is a gate confirming an absence
  rather than a gate checking an assertion.
- **No `px` type size and no raw colour.** Every size is a step on the type scale and every
  colour is a semantic role, so a reader who raises their text size moves the whole card and
  a theme can be re-derived for dark mode without touching this file.
- **No class that resolves to nothing in the product.** The rig knows which roles exist only
  in the documentation chrome and fails on them, which is the defect that looks correct in
  review because the preview above renders inside this page.
- **No banned word in copy or in an identifier**, and none of the eight words that can only
  be about a person in a comment that ships. The comment half of the scan is deliberately
  narrower than the full union, because a comment about code uses *failed*, *just* and
  *positive* in their ordinary senses and a rule that reports those is a rule somebody
  switches off. That is why the claim is made here at the width the rig actually runs at. It
  covers every string this component owns: three timing phrases, three admissions and one
  visually-hidden qualifier.

**Argued, and true of the implementation, but not yet measured:**

- **No category tint on the surface**, and this belongs here rather than in the list above.
  The axis rule fails an element that resolves *both* axes, so a card painted only from the
  category axis would pass the rig clean and the gate is not what backs this. What backs it
  is the file: it names no `--opsin-category-*` role, no `bg-category-*` utility and no
  `data-category`, and `Card` accepts neither a `status` nor a `category` prop, so there is
  no element on this card that could take a category colour in the first place.
- **Urgency is in the text.** The three timings render at the same type step, in the same
  ink, in the same position, with no glyph and no rule. Nothing about the timing is carried
  by colour, icon, weight or position, so greyscale, colour-vision deficiency and a text-only
  rendering lose nothing.
- **The card is a region named by its heading**, so it can be found by region or heading
  navigation without reading the page. The name comes from `aria-label` rather than
  `aria-labelledby`, and the cost is stated rather than hidden: pointing at the heading needs
  an id, generating one needs `useId`, and `useId` is a hook that would make this a client
  component for the sake of an attribute. A screen reader therefore says the instruction on
  entering the region and again when it reaches the heading.
- **Reading order is heading, status, reason, attribution, deadline, actions**, and the DOM
  is in that order. Nothing is re-arranged by CSS. The pill sits between the heading and the
  reason because it qualifies the thing the reason names; the specification's stated order
  lists the five parts of the tree and does not place it.
- **One tab stop per action and none for the card.** The card is not focusable. A region a
  keyboard user has to pass through on every card in a list is a tax with no payment.
- **The actions carry the 44pt floor** from `--opsin-target-minimum`, which is `rem` and so
  grows with the reader's text size rather than pinning at 44 device pixels, and they are
  kept apart by `--opsin-target-separation`, which carries the same `var()` fallback the
  floor does. A project that installed this file without the token sheet therefore gets a
  real gap rather than none, which is the adjacent-target case. The row wraps rather than
  shrinking, so at 200% text the two controls stack and keep their floor. Reasoned from the
  CSS; nobody has looked at it at 200% in either theme
  themes; the layout half of the rig runs nightly against a deployed URL and has not yet run
  against this component. See
  [Target size and motor](../accessibility/target-size-and-motor.mdx).
- **The leading action is marked in words as well as in fill.** Where two controls render,
  the recommended one carries a visually-hidden *the step this card is asking for* after its
  label. The qualifier is appended rather than substituted, so the visible label is still
  contained in the accessible name and voice control still matches what is written on the
  control. Without it the emphasis is a fill and nothing else, and a listener hears two
  identical links. It is not rendered on a card with one action, where there is nothing to
  tell it apart from. The flag never reorders the controls, so where a caller flags the
  second action the position points at the first one and these words are what correct it.
- **A localised deadline declares its own language.** `locale` formats the date and nothing
  else. *Due by*, the three timing phrases and the admissions are English whatever it says.
  The `time` element therefore carries `lang` whenever a locale was supplied, and a card
  localised into French does not have French words read out in an English voice. Nothing is
  stamped when no locale was given, because the document's own language is the right answer
  then and the runtime's default is not.
- **A missing `statusOf` warns where a missing `attribution` also speaks**, and the asymmetry
  is deliberate rather than an oversight. A card with no author makes a false claim. It reads
  as advice from a clinician, so the card corrects it on screen. A pill with no subject makes
  no false claim; it degrades an announcement, and the only repair available to a component
  would be to invent a subject, which is the substituted default this system refuses
  everywhere else. So that one is a development warning and a documented hazard, and not a
  sentence on the card.
- **A passed deadline is stated in words**, never by a colour change, and the date itself is
  written out rather than given as a relative phrase. A written date says the same thing to a
  reader who opened the screen an hour ago.
- **No motion and no announcement.** There is no entrance animation, no transition and no
  live region in this file, so the card cannot appear with movement that implies urgency and
  cannot speak over what the reader is doing. It never moves focus. See
  [Motion in health UI](../health/motion-in-health-ui.mdx).
- **Print renders the whole card**, including attribution and deadline. Nothing is hidden at
  print time, and `Card` grows a real border because a printer drops the fill and the inset
  edge the material paints. These cards are taken to appointments, and the author is the part
  that must survive.

**Not yet checked at all, and listed rather than omitted:**

- Contrast for the two action tones against the card, and for the timing phrase against the
  surface, in both themes. `prefers-contrast` and `forced-colors` are not implemented
  anywhere in opsinjs yet, so no increased-contrast claim can currently be made by anyone.
- Whether "region, then heading" reads as orientation or as duplication. Nobody has listened
  to this card in a screen reader.
- The telephone case. A `tel:` action is a plain link and has been reasoned about rather than
  tested on a phone, which for the one instruction that has to work first time is not enough.
- The copy this component owns is English, and there is no way to translate it. The three
  timing phrases and the admissions are fixed strings, and no prop supplies replacements.
  That is deliberate, because a prop that could relabel *Do this today* could also make it
  more insistent. Only the deadline follows `locale`. So a reader who cannot read English
  loses the urgency entirely, by construction, and nobody has designed the way out of that.
- The two action forms have never been seen next to each other. Every example on this page
  uses `href`, so nothing here renders the `onSelect` form, and the claim that a link and a
  button read alike is an intention this component is built to rather than an observation
  anybody has made.

## API reference

<PropsTable name="CareCardProps" />

`CareUrgency` and `CareAction` are exported from the same file. Neither appears in the table
above, which is generated only from interfaces whose name ends in `Props`.

```tsx
export type CareUrgency = "when-convenient" | "this-week" | "today"

export interface CareAction {
  label: string
  href?: string
  onSelect?: () => void
  recommended?: boolean
}
```

An action with an `href` renders as a link and one with `onSelect` renders as a button; the
two are built to read alike, because a reader has no way to interpret two controls that look
different only because one of them navigates. Built to, and not yet checked on screen: no
example on this page uses `onSelect`, so the two have never been rendered side by side.
Given both, the link wins. A destination survives a new tab, a copied address and a screen
reader's list of links, and a handler does not.
