A divider is the smallest component in the system: a border and a spacing token, drawn as a neutral hairline. It earns a component of its own for one reason only, which is the labelled case. A line with a word centred on it looks like the simplest thing on the page and is the easiest to get wrong, because a screen reader cannot be told a boundary has a name through the role that draws a boundary. So the labelled divider is quietly a different structure, and this page is mostly about that difference and about the colour a divider must never carry.

## When to use it

<WhenToUse
  use={[
    "Between two stacked groups of content that are already distinct, to make the seam between them visible without giving either group a surface of its own.",

    "As a thin vertical rule between a few inline facts in a row, inside a flex container that gives the rule its height.",
  ]}
  avoid={[
    { case: "You are grouping content that needs its own background, edge or padding, rather than a line between peers.", instead: "card" },
    { case: "You are separating layers of the interface by depth, such as a panel above the page, rather than drawing a line between things at the same level.", instead: "surface" },
    { case: "You are offering a choice between mutually exclusive views and the dividing lines are really the seams between options.", instead: "segmented-control" },
  ]}
/>

A divider draws a line and asserts nothing else. It does not create a group, it marks a seam in a group that already exists, so reach for it after the grouping is real rather than instead of making it real. If the line is the only thing telling the two sides apart, the two sides are not yet apart, and a card or a heading is the fix. The vertical form is the one that gets dropped into a layout that gives it no height and then looks broken; put it in a flex row, or give it a height through className, and it draws.

## Examples

### A labelled boundary in a list

The label marks the seam between today's items and the earlier ones, and it is the visible echo of a grouping the two lists already carry. Turn styles off and the two &lt;ul&gt; elements still separate the items; the divider is what a sighted reader gets on top of that, not instead of it.

### A vertical rule between inline facts

Three short labels in a row, told apart by a thin vertical hairline that takes its height from the row. The rule draws only because its flex container gives it a cross-axis height; drop the same rule into a block with no height and it collapses to nothing.

## Content guidelines

Keep a divider label to a short plain noun for the boundary it marks, such as "Earlier", "Today" or "Archived". It is a label, not a heading and not a sentence, so it takes no full stop and no verb. Do not make a divider carry meaning colour can carry better elsewhere: a red line does not mean "urgent" and a blue line does not mean "cardiac", it just reads as a line the reader cannot explain. Most of all, never let a divider be the only thing that separates two groups for a reader who cannot see it. Group the content with a list, a heading or a region first, then add the divider as the visible seam.

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    **A divider between two lists that already carry their own structure.** The seam is visible to a sighted reader and the grouping is real for everyone else, so the line is an echo rather than the only signal.
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    **A divider dropped between two runs of paragraphs to imply they are separate sections.** A screen reader hears one long run of text with a decorative line it cannot report, so the grouping the line promised never reaches half the readers.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

## Accessibility

A divider is not focusable and captures no key, so most of accessibility here is about what it says to a screen reader rather than about keyboard behaviour. An unlabelled divider exposes role="separator" with aria-orientation, which a reader can use to skip by region. A labelled divider deliberately drops that role, because a separator cannot carry an accessible name, and presents its label as ordinary text with two decorative hairlines that are hidden from assistive technology. The consequence is the one rule to hold onto: a divider is never the only carrier of grouping meaning for a screen reader, so the groups either side of it must be separated by structure as well. Nobody has yet listened to this component in a screen reader, which is why that claim is argued rather than measured.

## API reference

<PropsTable name="DividerProps" />

orientation defaults to horizontal, the form that needs nothing from its container. A value outside the two known ones is drawn horizontal rather than guessed at, with a development warning. label applies to the horizontal form only: supplied with a vertical orientation it is dropped, again with a warning, because a centred label between two vertical rules has no sound layout. className is the only route by which colour can reach the component, and the two colour axes stay off it.

**Easily confused with**

- [Card](./card.mdx) groups content by giving it a background, an edge and padding. A divider only draws a line between things that are already grouped.
- [Surface](./surface.mdx) places a group on its own material at a chosen depth. A divider marks a seam between peers at the same level.
- [SegmentedControl](./segmented-control.mdx) offers a choice between mutually exclusive views. Its seams look like dividers but they carry a selection, which a divider never does.
