RangeBar is the component the rest of opsinjs is arranged around, and it is the one to
read first if you are deciding whether this system is worth building on. It is also the
most dangerous, because the distance between *here is your number against its range* and
*here is a verdict about your health* is one careless colour choice wide.

The sentence is the component and the bar is a drawing of the sentence. `RangeBar.Summary`
states the reading, the range, the position and whose range it is. Where there is no range
it can honestly compare against, the summary says so and stops instead. Either sentence is
rendered in words, on every surface and at every size. Everything else on this page exists
to draw that sentence for people who would rather see it than read it.

## Preview

Two bars: a reading against a range, and the same reading with no range to compare it
with. The second is the one worth looking at, because it is the case products get wrong.
The tempting repair is a typical adult band and a small asterisk, and a reader cannot tell
a borrowed band from a real one.

Open it under the product theme and turn the text size up. The claim that this component
survives without colour is meant to be checked rather than believed: in greyscale the

The distinction that matters most is the first one. A reference range is not a property of
a measurement; it is a property of a measurement *and a population and a laboratory*. If
you cannot say whose range it is, RangeBar is the wrong component, because drawing a band
on a line is a much stronger claim than printing two numbers in small type. Use
[Value](./value.mdx) and say plainly that no range is available.

You can also hand RangeBar a reading with no range and let it say that for you. It renders
the number, states in the summary that there is nothing to compare it with, and draws no
bar at all. That is the same message, in the component the rest of the screen is already
using.

## Clinical meaning

**What it asserts.** RangeBar asserts exactly one thing: *this measurement, expressed in
this unit, sits at this position relative to this reference range, which came from this
source*. It asserts nothing about cause, nothing about trend, and nothing about what
happens next.

**What it must never be read as.** It is not a diagnosis, not a risk score, and not a
verdict. A value outside the range is a value outside the range; it is not "bad", it is not
"abnormal", and it is emphatically not "normal" when it is inside. That word is banned
throughout opsinjs and [Reference ranges and normal](../health/reference-ranges.mdx)
explains why. Most people fall outside at least one reference range at any given time, and
a UI that treats *outside* as *wrong* generates far more anxiety than information.

**What it computes, and it is a short list.** A position along a line, and one of the three
words *within*, *above* and *below* for that position, against the bounds the caller
supplied. That is arithmetic on the product's own numbers, and it is the same arithmetic
that decides where the tick goes. It never becomes a level of attention: `status` is an
input, and removing it changes the colour of the tick and nothing else.

It computes that word only where there is something to compute it against. *Within* is the
answer arithmetic falls through to, and *within the range* against an interval nobody
supplied is a reassurance derived from nothing, so a range carrying a source and neither
bound, or bounds that run downwards, is discarded rather than half-used. The sentence in
that case is the one the component gives for a reading with no range at all.

**Which colour axis it may use.** The **status** axis only, and only when the consuming
product has supplied a status. RangeBar may never derive a status from position on its own
bar: *outside the range* and *needs attention* are different claims, and only a clinician
or a validated rule can convert one into the other. It may never use the **category** axis
to fill the bar. A heart-red bar and an attention-red bar side by side make the two axes
indistinguishable, which is the single failure mode
[The two colour axes](../health/two-colour-axes.mdx) exists to prevent.

The implementation keeps them apart by element rather than by convention. `category` tints
`RangeBar.Label` and stamps `data-category` there; `status` colours the StatusPill and
`RangeBar.Tick` and stamps `data-status` on both; the root, the track and the band take
colour from neither. No
element in the rendered DOM carries both attributes, which is what the axis-conflict gate
checks and what makes a bar safe to drop into a category-tinted card.

**Who owns the thresholds.** The consuming product, always. RangeBar takes a range and a
status as inputs and has no table of its own; there is no built-in set of reference ranges
in opsinjs and there will never be one. Shipping thresholds inside a component library
would mean shipping clinical decisions to every product that installs it, silently, with no
review and no version story. The same rule is why there is no `staleAfterHours` here: how
old is too old is clinical, and a component that guessed would have decided, on behalf of a
product that never asked, when to stop telling somebody their result is current.

**Recency and staleness are different obligations, and only the second one is clinical.**
Not owning a staleness boundary is not a reason to say nothing about time. A number on a
screen with no time beside it is read as *now*, so the footnote states when the reading was
taken. Where `measuredAt` was not supplied, the footnote states that nobody knows. That is
a fact about the data, and it is stated because a reading is a number the reader owns.
An undated range is the other case and stays quiet, because its author is already named in
the sentence above the bar, so a confession of a date the reader could not act on would
add nothing. Deciding
when that fact should start alarming somebody is the part that belongs to the product, and
[RelativeTime](./relative-time.mdx) is where a product brings its own boundary.

**Provenance class is not here at all, and a screen made only of bars cannot claim it.**
RangeBar has no prop for *measured by a cuff*, *entered by you*, *estimated from another
figure*, and `range.source` attributes the range rather than the reading. Group E of the
[safety review checklist](../health/safety-review-checklist.mdx) asks for every value's
provenance class in plain words, and this component cannot answer it; a surface that needs
to must carry it alongside, from
[Data provenance and device accuracy](../health/data-provenance-and-device-accuracy.mdx).

**Colour is never the only carrier.** Position on the bar, the tick and its numeric label,
the two boundary numbers and the text summary all carry the same information
independently, so the component survives greyscale and all three common forms of
colour-vision deficiency. Nothing here has been put on paper, because opsinjs has no print
stylesheet. Read the print case as an intent rather than a result. Where a status has been
supplied, its word and its glyph sit beside the label and outside the summary, so that a
caller replacing the sentence cannot take the word with it. See
[Colour independence](../accessibility/colour-independence.mdx).

**A bar needs two bounds.** A one-sided range such as *up to 20* or *10 and upwards* has no
width, and a width is what a scale is made of. Given one bound, RangeBar draws no track at
all and states the comparison in words instead; the alternative is to invent the other end
of the line, and a scale nobody chose is indistinguishable, on screen, from one a
laboratory published.

**A reading far outside its range compresses the band, and that is the honest picture.**
The drawn extent is a band-width wider than the range on each side, and it always stretches
to contain the reading. A value ten times the top of the range therefore leaves the band a
sliver near one end. It is drawn to scale because it is true to scale: a bar that clipped
the reading to the edge would be showing it somewhere it is not, and the numbers are on the
marks and in the sentence either way.

<SafetyCallout severity="urgent" evidence="opinion">
If the reference range is unknown, RangeBar renders the value with an explicit *no
reference range available* state and no band at all. It never falls back to a default
range, a population average, or a range inferred from the person's own history. A plausible
band is worse than no band, because the reader cannot tell the difference. A `range` that
arrives with an empty `source` is reported as OPSIN-0004 and not drawn. An unattributed
band is a comparison with no author. A `range` carrying a source but neither bound, or
bounds that run downwards, is discarded the same way and reported in development: the
sentence says there is nothing to compare the reading with, rather than stating a position
against an interval that was never supplied and is printed nowhere.
</SafetyCallout>

<ResearchNote evidence="mixed" date="2026-09-02">
The design bet is that a value drawn against its range on a line is understood more
reliably by non-specialists than the same value printed beside its range, or flagged H and
L in a table. That bet rests on the published literature on presenting laboratory results
to patients. Two anchors worth reading before you argue with this page: the 2024 systematic
review of result-presentation formats in JMIR (doi:10.2196/53993), and the 2018 study of
patient portal result presentation in BMC Medical Informatics and Decision Making
(doi:10.1186/s12911-018-0589-7). Both are reviews of a body of work rather than a single
trial, and both are the right starting point for checking this claim rather than taking it
from us.

What is **evidence** here is the broad direction: visual, positional formats support
comprehension better than bare numbers for readers with low numeracy. What is **opinion**,
and marked as such, is every specific decision on this page, including the refusal to
derive status from position, the treatment of an unknown range as a first-class state, and
the choice to put the numeric value adjacent to the tick rather than in a legend. Those are
our judgements. Nobody has run a trial on them.
</ResearchNote>

The parts are `data-slot` attributes, not exports. There is no `RangeBar.Track` you can
import, and the bar is not composable: which parts exist is decided by the data the
component was given rather than by how a caller nested it, and it cannot render a tick
outside a track, because there is no way to hand it one.

The slots are for restyling, and one of them is not for hiding. `display: none` on
`[data-slot="range-bar-summary"]` takes the sentence out of the accessibility tree and
leaves a picture whose entire meaning is a position on a line. That picture is hidden from
the accessibility tree with `aria-hidden`, so what remains for a non-visual reader is
nothing at all. No prop can do this; only a stylesheet can, and this is the page asking you
not to. A dashboard row
that genuinely needs a compact form wants [MetricTile](./metric-tile.mdx), which is the
component for that width and carries its own words.

The level is delivered by the system's [StatusPill](./status-pill.mdx), rendered outside
the summary sentence so a caller replacing the summary cannot take the word away. It sits
outside the sentence for a second reason too: the status word has to be redundant with the
colour, because the measured CVD audit in `tokens/color.json` finds *steady* and
*attention* identical under deuteranopia and in greyscale, and the pill carries a word and
a distinct glyph alongside its fill. RangeBar once painted its own status word and glyph
here. It now shares the one pill every status-bearing component renders, so a reader meets
the same object for the same assertion across the whole system rather than a bar-only
variant of it.

product owns. Delete it and the same tick is drawn in a neutral tone.

### A range with only one bound

*Up to 20* has no width, so there is no scale and no bar. The sentence carries the whole
comparison instead, and the picture that would have been drawn from an invented second
bound is simply not drawn.

The sentence also changes shape. On a two-bound bar the position word leads: *within the
range 10 to 20 mg/dL*. With one bound that frame collapses, because *within the range up to
20* sets two prepositions fighting over one number. So a one-bound range names the bound and
the direction from it. A high-only range reads *at or under the upper limit of 20 mg/dL* for
a reading on or below the limit and *higher than the upper limit of 20 mg/dL* for one above
it; a low-only range mirrors that with *at or above the lower limit of 10 mg/dL* and *lower
than the lower limit of 10 mg/dL*. Those are the forms to reach for when you replace the
`summary`. The preview below is high-only, so the sentence is the whole of it.

### A reading far above the range

The drawn extent always stretches to contain the reading, so a value at twice the top of
the range squeezes the band towards a sliver at one end. That compression is the
information rather than a fault: a number a long way outside its range should look a long
way outside it, and widening the band to sit comfortably would draw a scale nobody chose.
The band stays where the numbers put it, the marks keep their true positions, and the
component states a position and still no verdict.

## States

RangeBar is not interactive. Nothing in it takes focus, it answers no pointer or
keyboard event, and it has no hover, pressed or disabled appearance, because it is a
drawing of a reading somebody else assigned rather than a control. The picture is hidden
from assistive technology and the summary sentence beside it is the whole announcement.
So the states worth naming are the five data states, read here against what the component
was actually handed.

| Data state | What RangeBar renders |
| --- | --- |
| Error | It owns no fetch, so an upstream error belongs to the surface that composes it. A range handed in with no `source`, with neither bound, or with bounds that run downwards is not an interval: it is discarded, the summary states there is nothing to compare the reading with, and the defect is reported in development. |
| Loading | No skeleton and no spinner. It renders synchronously from the props it is given, so the composing surface owns any loading affordance while the reading is still being fetched. |
| Empty | A `value` of `null` is a first-class empty state, distinct from `0`: no tick is drawn, and the summary names the metric and states its range where one was supplied rather than placing a mark at zero. |
| Partial | A reading with no range, or a range with only one bound, draws no track. The summary carries the whole comparison in words, and the picture that would need an invented second bound is not drawn. |
| Stale | It states when the reading was taken, and says outright that nobody knows where `measuredAt` is absent, because a number with no time is read as now. It owns no staleness boundary, because how old is too old is clinical: a surface that needs one wraps the reading in [RelativeTime](./relative-time.mdx). |

## Content guidelines

The label is what was measured, in the reader's words. Prefer the everyday name and give
the clinical one as a [Term](./term.mdx) if the reader will meet it on a letter from a
clinic. The summary sentence states position, never verdict, and always includes both the
value and the range so the sentence stands alone when it is read out of context in a
screen reader, in a notification or in a printed summary.

Keep the summary under about twenty words. It is read aloud, and a long sentence is a
sentence somebody interrupts. The generated one is built to that budget: subject, reading,
position, range, source, stop. Replace it with `summary` when your unit or your reader
needs different phrasing. Even then you are replacing the words, not the sentence. The
component renders whatever you pass once, as the visible sentence beside the bar, which is
also what a screen reader reads, because the picture is hidden.

**Precision is decimal places, not significant figures**, and it belongs to the metric
rather than to the value. The same metric shows the same number of decimal places every
time, so two readings can be compared at a glance. It applies to the reading and to both
boundary numbers alike, because a value and the bound it is being compared with are the
same measurement. If a range is narrow enough that both marks round to the same number, the
component labels them identically and says so in a development warning: the marks are in
the right places and the metric's stated precision cannot separate its own bounds. Adding a
decimal place to pull them apart would be a claim about accuracy that nobody made, so
nothing here does it for you. See
[Numbers, units and precision](../health/numbers-units-precision.mdx).

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    **"Example measurement is 26 mg/dL, above the range 10 to 20 mg/dL, from Example data.
    Not a reference range."** States the value, states the range, names whose range
    it is, states the position, stops. Leaves the interpretation to the person who is
    qualified to give it. It is also the sentence the component generates unaided. The
    numbers and the source are the synthetic ones every example in opsinjs uses: your
    screen puts a real reading and your laboratory's own attributed range into the same
    slots, and this page will never print either of those for you.
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    **"Example measurement is 26 mg/dL. Abnormal. Your result is poor."** Two failures.
    It converts a position into a verdict the component has no basis for, and it converts
    a verdict into a judgement about the reader. Neither is RangeBar's to make.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

<DoDont>
  <DoDont.Do>
    **"We do not have a reference range for this test, so we cannot show where this result
    sits."** Says what is missing and why the picture is not there. The reader learns
    something true, and it is what the component renders on its own when `range` is
    omitted.
  </DoDont.Do>
  <DoDont.Dont>
    **A bar drawn with a typical adult range and a small asterisk.** The asterisk is not
    read. The band is. Once a band is on screen the reader has already formed a judgement
    about a range that may not be theirs.
  </DoDont.Dont>
</DoDont>

The two words people reach for hardest here are **normal** and **abnormal**, and
[Reference ranges and normal](../health/reference-ranges.mdx) is the argument for why
*within the usual range* is both kinder and more accurate. That page carries the whole
banned list, with what to write instead of each one, and it is longer than the two above.
`elevated` and `positive` are on it, and both are words an author writes into a sentence
about a reading above a range without noticing. Read the list there rather than a copy of
it here: a hand-restated list drifts from the one the build actually enforces, and the
enforced one is generated from `tokens/glossary.json` and that table.

## Motion

RangeBar declares no transition, no animation and no first-paint movement. The tick does
not slide into position, the band does not grow, and the number does not count up, so
there is nothing for `prefers-reduced-motion` to reduce and nothing read from
`tokens/motion.json`. That silence is deliberate for a health value: a reading that arrives
by animation has shown, for every frame of the journey, a figure that is not true. The
reading is drawn where the numbers put it, at once, and it stays there.

## Accessibility

Some of this is enforced by a gate on every pull request and every push to `main`. The
rest is argued, and it is labelled as such:
`beta` promises that accessibility is claimed only where it has been checked, with the
gaps listed rather than left out. Nothing on this page has been through a review with a
disabled user, and `a11yDate` is therefore still empty.

**Checked, by `pnpm run check:a11y` on every pull request and every push to `main`:**

- **Colour is never the only carrier of a status.** The gate fails the build on a file that
  paints a status colour without also reading `CLINICAL_STATUS_META` and a lucide glyph, and
  on any status surface with no `data-status`. This component reads the word and the icon
  name from that table and never writes either as a literal, so the four levels are four
  distinct shapes rather than one shape in four colours. Four distinct shapes are what the
  measured CVD audit in `tokens/color.json` requires. See
  [Health glyphs](../foundations/iconography/health-glyphs.mdx).
- **No element takes colour from both axes.** The same gate reads every JSX element and
  fails on one that carries both `data-category` and `data-status`, or that writes a
  category colour and a status colour into the same opening tag. It is the attribute half
  that does the work here: this file keeps every colour class in a module-level lookup
  keyed on the level or the category, and the gate reads the tag rather than resolving the
  table behind it. Both axis-bearing elements do carry their attribute, so a mixture would
  trip it. What is checked is still the attributes, and the colours are checked by reading
  the file.
- **No shipped threshold.** Two rules, and they catch the same defect spelled two ways. A
  name matching *stale*, *threshold*, *cutoff*, *limit*, *minimum*, *maximum*, *expiry*,
  *band* or *plausib* given a numeric literal is an error anywhere under `registry/`; so is
  an object literal setting two numeric bounds without a `source` beside them in the same
  literal. The pairs it looks for are `low`/`high`, `from`/`to`, `lower`/`upper` and
  `start`/`end`. There is no range, no staleness boundary and no plausibility bound in this
  component or in any of its examples: every interval on screen arrived as a prop,
  and every one in an example names `EXAMPLE_SOURCE`.
- **No type size in pixels, and no raw colour literal**, so the whole component scales with
  the reader's own text size and takes every colour from the two axes and the eleven
  bridged surface roles.

**Argued, and true of the implementation, but not yet measured:**

- **The summary is in the accessibility tree at every size**, as ordinary visible text
  rather than a hidden label. Where a usable range was supplied it states the reading, its
  unit, the range, the position and whose range it is; where one was not, whether because
  none was given or because the component discarded the one that arrived, it states the
  reading and says there is nothing to compare it with. It never states a position without
  printing the interval that position is against. A reader who cannot see the bar loses the
  picture and nothing else.
- **The bar is not an interactive slider, and it is hidden from assistive technology.**
  `RangeBar.Track` carries `aria-hidden`, so the picture is out of the accessibility tree
  rather than announced as a named graphic. It is not focusable, is not read as a `slider`,
  a `meter` or a `progressbar`, and it is not in the tab order. All three of those roles
  promise a value the reader can inspect or change, and this is a drawing of a reading
  somebody else assigned. A non-visual reader meets the summary sentence beneath the bar
  instead, which is the component in words: it is present at every text size, on paper and
  in a copy and paste, and it is spoken once rather than twice now that the picture no
  longer quotes it under its own name. The level rides on the visible StatusPill above the
  bar rather than on the picture's name, so a caller who replaces the summary cannot take
  the status word away with it.
- **The status colour never travels alone.** The tick takes the status colour, and the tick
  is inside the picture, which carries `aria-hidden`, so that colour is hidden from the
  accessibility tree along with everything else in the image. The level a non-visual reader
  hears is the word on the visible StatusPill above the bar, which is in the tree whenever
  `level` is set and is never removed when a caller replaces `summary`. Colour is the
  picture's only carrier of status, and the picture is exactly what a screen reader does not
  meet.
- **The unit is spoken, not spelled.** Every number goes through
  [Value](./value.mdx), which substitutes the unit's spoken form from `tokens/units.json`:
  a listener hears *milligrams per decilitre* rather than the symbol read out letter by
  letter.
- **At 200% text the component is already stacked.** The summary sits under the track and
  the boundary labels sit beneath their marks, and the space reserved for those labels is
  set in `em`, so it grows with them. Nothing is truncated and no ellipsis is set anywhere
  in the file. Checked by eye; not checked by a machine.
- **Nothing animates.** There is no transition on the tick, no growth on the band and no
  count on the number, on first paint or afterwards. A health value that arrives by
  animation has displayed a figure that is not true for every frame of the journey.

**Not checked at all, and listed rather than omitted:**

- Every contrast pair: the tick against the band, the tick against the track, the band
  against the track, the status word against whatever is behind it, and the boundary
  labels. `scripts/check-contrast.mts` has not been run for this component, which is why
  the report below is empty rather than reassuring. Two of those pairs are known to need
  the machine rather than an argument. The band's outline is drawn from the same ink as the
  boundary marks rather than from the theme's hairline, because `lib/generated/contrast.json`
  records a neutral hairline on the page at APCA Lc 22.42 and WCAG 1.47:1 against a
  non-text floor of Lc 45 and 3:1. The new pairing is better and is still unmeasured. And
  the status word and the category tint are `ink` roles, which `tokens/color.json` tunes
  against the matching `-surface`; this component sets no surface of its own, so they are
  painted on the host's background instead, and that is a different pairing from the one
  the token contract guarantees.
- How the hidden picture actually plays for a screen-reader user. The graphic now carries
  `aria-hidden`, so the summary sentence is heard once and the drawing is skipped, which is
  the arrangement the charts doctrine permits when the text twin sits beside it. Whether a
  non-visual reader would rather have the picture named in a rotor than skipped has not been
  listened to by anybody.
- Print. The band is outlined and the tick is a solid mark, which is what a printer
  dropping backgrounds leaves standing. Set against that, opsinjs has no print stylesheet,
  none of this has been put on paper, and the one print rule in the docs chrome keys on
  `data-status`, which the band does not carry. Treat the outline as an intent.
- What the whole bar draws under `forced-colors: active`. The tick keeps a fill by asking
  for the system `Highlight` colour and carries a `CanvasText` outline on top of it, so the
  reading's position survives where a fill alone would be stripped; the two boundary marks
  take `CanvasText` by the same route; and the band keeps the border it already has, so its
  extent stays drawn. The whole bar therefore holds together in the mode. The cost is that
  the four status levels collapse to the one `Highlight` colour there, so the tick can no
  longer tell them apart, and the word on the StatusPill above the bar is what does, which
  is the rule the status axis keeps everywhere: the colour is never the only carrier. None
  of this has been put in front of a machine or a high-contrast user, which is why it sits
  in this list rather than the checked one.
- `prefers-contrast: more` is implemented in [Surface](./surface.mdx#accessibility) alone
  rather than in the token layer, and this bar renders no Surface, so that mode changes
  nothing here. `forced-colors: active` is not a token-layer feature either, so what the bar
  draws in it is the tick's own outline, the boundary marks and the band border set out in
  the bullet above, not a token a reader could count on elsewhere. None of it has been
  measured under either mode.
- What the two rows of labels do at very large text sizes. Each boundary label grows
  outward from its mark rather than centring on it: the low label occupies only the space
  before its mark and the high label only the space after its, so the two can never overlap
  however narrow the band becomes, and safe alignment holds each one inside the track's box
  rather than letting it hang past the component and scroll the page sideways, which is the
  reflow break [Text resizing and zoom](../accessibility/text-resizing-and-zoom.mdx) treats
  as a failure. The reading label above the track is centred on its mark but clamped, its
  centre kept a declared half-label allowance from either edge: `--rb-label-half` is 3.5em,
  so a label up to 7em wide stays inside the track. What is not checked is that none of this
  has been measured by a machine, and that a unit wider than the allowance still overhangs
  the track by the excess, with the tick keeping the true position underneath it.

## API reference

<PropsTable name="RangeBarProps" />

Three things differ from the interface this page proposed while it was unbuilt, and each of
them is a decision rather than an oversight. `precision` is **decimal places**, not
significant figures, and it does not sniff the precision of the float it was handed.
Significant figures would show one metric to different numbers of decimal places at
different magnitudes. It is required, so a TypeScript caller cannot omit it and let a raw
double reach a reader; required is not the same as defaulted, because the component still
supplies no number of its own, and a JavaScript caller who omits it gets the honest
fallback of nothing rounded and nothing padded. `category` is typed to the six health categories rather than to
`string`, for the reason the page already gave about `status`: a component that accepts an
arbitrary category accepts a seventh colour ramp that does not exist. And `measuredAt`
renders a date in the footnote and no staleness treatment at all, because a staleness
boundary is a clinical number opsinjs must not own. A surface that needs one wraps the
reading in [RelativeTime](./relative-time.mdx), which takes the boundary from you. Omitting
it does not buy silence: a number with no time is read as *now*. The footnote then says
outright that nobody knows when the reading was taken.

`locale` is new, and it is a pass-through: it reaches every `Value` this component renders
and the dates in the footnote, so one bar cannot show two conventions. The demo on this
page fixes `en-GB` so its capture reads the date day first, as "14 March 2026". A product
passes the locale the surface renders in. Omitting it hands formatting to whichever
environment renders the bar, which is the server process on a server render and the
reader's browser after hydration, so the two disagree wherever their locales differ.

`ClinicalStatus` is the four-level union shared by every status-bearing component in the
system. [Clinical status semantics](../health/clinical-status-semantics.mdx) is where those
four levels are defined. It is deliberately not a `string`: a component that accepts an
arbitrary status string accepts `"critical"`, `"abnormal"` and `"bad"` on day one, and the
vocabulary is gone. A value outside the four is refused with a development warning and the
bar is drawn without a status, rather than approximated into one.

`ReferenceRange` is declared once, in `lib/opsinjs.ts`, and shared with every other
component that compares a reading against an interval. Both bounds are optional because a
one-sided range is a real range; `source` is not, because an unattributed band is an
assertion with no author. The type therefore permits two shapes that are not intervals. One
supplies neither bound, and the other has bounds that run downwards. RangeBar discards
both, with a development warning, rather than comparing a reading against them. A one-sided
range is still compared and still drawn as words.
