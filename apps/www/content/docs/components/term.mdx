Term is small, unglamorous and probably the component with the highest ratio of benefit to
effort in the whole system. Most health interfaces are readable except for four words per
screen, and those four words are why people stop reading.

The first exclusion is the one that matters. Term is not a licence to keep the jargon. The
question is always *does this reader have to learn this word?* The answer is yes only when
the word will reach them from somewhere the product does not control. Everywhere else, say
it plainly and skip the component.

## Clinical meaning

**What it asserts.** That this word has this everyday meaning, in this context, according to
the product's glossary. It is a translation claim, and it is the product's claim, not a
clinician's. opsinjs supplies no definitions for a product to use: the component arrives
with an empty glossary and a product hands it a full one. Two definitions do travel inside
the file, because the zero-prop demo at the foot of `term.tsx` has to have something to
define; they are unexported and no call site outside that demo can reach them. That is the
exception to the sentence, stated rather than left for somebody to find in the source.

**What it must never be read as.** A definition of the reader's condition, or advice.
*Chronic means long-lasting* is a translation; *chronic means you will have this for life*
is a prognosis, and the glossary is not the place for one. Definitions describe words.

**Which colour axis it may use.** Neither. A term is typographic. The mark is a dotted
underline, which survives greyscale, increased contrast and a black-and-white printout.
Tinting jargon by category makes words look like statuses, and tinting them by status is
worse: a word in the `urgent` ramp is a clinical level that nobody assigned and nothing
counts against the escalation budget.

**The glossary is the product's, and it is data.** In this repository the definitions live
in `tokens/glossary.json`, are written in plain English, and are versioned and reviewable
like any other content. They are never generated at runtime, never fetched from a third
party, and never copied from a national health service. The NHS A to Z is Crown copyright
and is not reusable, however tempting the shortcut. Cite sources; never paste them. See
[Plain-English A to Z](../content/plain-english-a-z.mdx).

**One entry can refuse to be shown.** A glossary entry marked `showBoth: "plain-only"` says
the clinical word is never put in front of a reader. The entry exists so an author can look
up what to write instead. Term honours that by rendering the plain wording as ordinary text
and marking nothing, and by saying so in development. There is nothing to explain when the
word on the screen is already the plain one. On that one path `children` is discarded rather
than printed: `children` is where a call site writes the clinical word inflected for its own
sentence, and printing it would be the component overruling the refusal it is implementing.
The sentence a reader gets is therefore not the sentence the author wrote, so the discard
warns in development, and the repair is to write the plain wording directly and drop the
component.

**The other two `showBoth` values are recorded and not implemented, and this page says so
rather than letting the type imply otherwise.** In the glossary, `always` means *show the
clinical word and the plain wording together every time*, and it exists for matching: a
reader holding a printout has to be able to tell it is the same measurement as the one on the
screen. Term chooses inline or disclosure from `present`, `once` and the length of the
definition and never from `showBoth`, so an `always` entry with a long definition still lands
behind a control with its plain wording out of sight. Obeying it would mean overriding the
call site's own `present` and `once`, which a component does not get to do to the surface it
is standing on. The mismatch therefore warns in development instead, and `present="inline"`
is how a call site asks for what the glossary asked for. `first-use` is the caller's to
express through `once`, for the same reason `once` is a prop at all: opsinjs cannot see
where one surface ends and the next begins.

**Where this component and the authored glossary disagree, and it is not settled.**
`tokens/glossary.json` carries a policy rule reading *plain wording is the default and the
clinical term is the annotation, never the other way around*, and it names the shape this
component implements as the thing not to do. That shape is a clinical word with its
explanation on demand. The counter-argument is in the same policy block, in its own note on
why `always` exists: matching a printout needs the clinical spelling visible on the screen.
Both sentences are authored, they contradict each other, and neither the component nor the
glossary settles it alone. The conflict is recorded here and at the top of `term.tsx`
rather than resolved quietly in favour of whichever was written second, and it is the first
thing to decide before this component leaves alpha.

<SafetyCallout severity="attention" evidence="opinion">
A definition that drifts into advice is the failure mode of this component, and it is not
caught by anything: the sentence still reads well, the underline still looks right, and the
product has quietly started telling somebody what to do about their own result through a
tooltip. *Acute means sudden, or short-lasting* is a definition. *Acute means this will
settle down on its own* is guidance with nobody behind it. Review the glossary as clinical
content, not as microcopy.
</SafetyCallout>

<ResearchNote evidence="opinion">
The definition should be readable by someone who has not finished secondary school, because
a large share of adults in any population find health information hard to use. A definition
that needs a second definition has failed, and *idiopathic: of unknown aetiology* is the
canonical example of failing.
</ResearchNote>

`Term.Definition` is never removed from the accessibility tree by a presentation choice. A
disclosure hides it visually; it does not hide it from a screen reader before it is opened,
because a reader using speech has no way to know something is there to open. That single
sentence is what rules out every disclosure primitive in `@base-ui/react`: `preview-card`
opens on hover and there is no hover on a phone, `popover` portals the definition out of the
sentence and hides it from assistive technology while closed, and `collapsible` keeps the
reading order but puts `hidden` on the closed panel, which is precisely what "removed from
the accessibility tree" means. The disclosure here is built by hand and built the other way
round: the definition is always exposed, and the control changes only whether it is on the
screen.

Because that control holds state, the whole file carries `"use client"`. A directive is
file-level and a component here is one flat file, so the boundary lands on all of it or none
of it. The inline presentation pays for a client boundary it does not need.

The parts are identified by `data-slot` and are styleable from outside, and none of them is
an export. Two render paths carry the root and nothing inside it, because both render an
unmarked word and there is nothing there to mark or to define. They are an `id` the glossary
does not hold and an entry marked `plain-only`. A product stylesheet or a product-side test
keyed on `[data-slot="term-trigger"]` matches nothing on those paths, which is why the tree
says `0-1` rather than `1` and why the note names them. Nothing else distinguishes the two:
the data-attribute vocabulary in this system is closed at four, and a fifth attribute to
make the absence addressable is a change to that contract rather than to this component.
Everywhere else the parts arrive together and there is no way to render one without the
others.

## Examples

### Reading a paragraph without leaving it

Two terms is already close to the ceiling for one paragraph. The third appearance is the
thing to watch: it carries `once`, so the word is still marked and the definition is still
one press away and still in the accessibility tree, and it is not printed a second time.

### An abbreviation needs two things

The expansion is what the letters stand for; the definition is what the thing is, and
neither substitutes for the other. `SpO2` also shows the third field: written down it is
read aloud as a word by speech synthesis, so the entry says how the letters are spoken and
the letters are added to the control's accessible name after the written form. The name is
*SpO2 S P O 2*. Added, not substituted. The written spelling is the one on the reader's own
paperwork, and taking it out of the accessibility tree would withhold it from precisely the
readers who cannot see it on the screen.

## Content guidelines

Definitions are one sentence, under about twenty words, in the second person where it helps.
Define the word, not the condition. Do not use a second piece of jargon inside a definition.
Do not hedge to the point of vacuity.

Expansions are the full form and nothing else: *eGFR* expands to *estimated glomerular
filtration rate*, and *an estimate of how well your kidneys are filtering* is the definition.
Most readers need both, in that order, and the component renders them in that order.

Length is not only a style question here. `auto` sends a definition inline when it is short
enough to share a line with the sentence it interrupts and behind a control when it is not,
so a definition written tightly is a definition the reader gets without pressing anything.

One class of definition needs deciding before it is written at all: the ones whose plain
wording turns into a verdict once it is spliced into a sentence about the reader's own
result. *Benign means not cancer* is a definition in *a report describing a finding as
benign* and is reassurance about a named person in *your biopsy was benign*, and the
component call is identical in both. Term renders whatever the glossary holds into whatever
sentence the product wrote and cannot tell those apart, so the surface has to: a word of
that kind belongs on a screen that carries a route to a person. See
[Delivering difficult results](../health/delivering-difficult-results.mdx).

## Accessibility

Some of this is checked by a gate on every pull request and every push to `main`. Most of it
is argued, and it is labelled as such: `alpha` promises that accessibility is claimed only
where it has been checked, with the gaps listed rather than left out.

**Checked, by `node scripts/check-a11y.mts` on every pull request and every push to `main`.
There is no commit hook in this repository, and a sentence that implied one would be
claiming a gate that does not run:**

- **No type size in px anywhere in this component.** The word, the definition and the
  control's padding are all relative, so a reader who has raised their text size gets a
  larger definition and a larger target with it. See
  [Dynamic type](../foundations/typography/dynamic-type.mdx).
- **No colour literal, no numbered ramp step, and no class that resolves in the docs chrome
  and to nothing in the product.** The gate proves this file contains no colour value it
  could paint the trigger with; it does not prove the underline is there, which is why the
  next section exists.

**Argued, and true of the implementation, but not measured:**

- **The definition is available without hover and without pointer precision.** Hover-only
  definitions do not exist on touch, do not exist for keyboard users, and are unreliable for
  anyone with a tremor. This is why `present` has no `hover` value.
- **The definition is in the accessibility tree whether or not it is open.** Collapsed, it is
  `display: none` on screen and reaches the reader as the trigger's accessible description.
  A node referenced by `aria-describedby` contributes its text whether or not it is
  displayed. A reader using speech therefore meets the definition without having to guess
  that a control is worth pressing. The reference is kept while the definition is open too,
  and that costs something worth naming: open, the definition is both the control's
  description and the text immediately after it, so a reader moving through the sentence
  meets it twice on every pass. Dropping the reference on opening would trade the repetition
  for a relationship that changes underneath the reader, which some assistive technology will
  not re-read. Neither answer is free, and this one is a choice rather than an oversight.
- **The word is never split from its definition in the reading order.** The definition is the
  trigger's next sibling. Nothing is portalled, and the component never moves focus.
- **The disclosure trigger is not marked by colour alone.** A dotted underline distinguishes
  it, and it is the only mark, so it survives greyscale and increased contrast. Its offset is
  in `em` rather than pixels, so the rule stays clear of the descenders of a word a reader
  has enlarged. The inline trigger carries no mark at all, and that is deliberate, because
  an underline pointing at a definition that is already on the screen promises something
  already delivered. This bullet is therefore a claim about the disclosure and not about
  every term.
- **The disclosure is a button** with `aria-expanded`, native Enter and Space activation, and
  an accessible name computed from its own content rather than replaced by an `aria-label`.
  That is what keeps the word on the screen inside the name, which is what SC 2.5.3 Label in
  Name asks for and what a speech-input user saying *click eGFR* needs in order to reach
  anything. The name is *eGFR*, or *SpO2 S P O 2* where the entry spells the letters out. An
  earlier version named the control *what does e G F R mean?*, which replaced the visible
  word rather than containing it and so did not meet that criterion on any disclosure this
  page renders. Escape closes it and consumes the key only while the definition is open;
  focus never left the control, so there is nothing to return.
- **Abbreviations that must be spelt out carry both forms.** An entry carrying `speech` puts
  the letters into the accessibility tree after the written word rather than in place of it,
  so a speech reader hears the synthesiser's attempt at the word and then the letters, and a
  braille reader still gets the spelling that is on their own paperwork. Substituting one for
  the other withholds that spelling from exactly the readers who cannot see it, and the
  spelling is what `word` exists for. That substitution is what this component used to do.
- **At 200% text an inline definition reflows** onto as many lines as it needs. Nothing sets
  a width, a fixed height, `white-space: nowrap` or an ellipsis, so there is nothing to
  truncate and nothing to scroll sideways.
- **Print expands the definition beside its word** and drops the underline, because a printed
  page has no disclosures and the definition is the part the reader takes to the appointment.
  An occurrence marked `once` stays closed on paper too.

**Not yet checked at all, and listed rather than omitted:**

- **The 44x44 hit area is not met, and this is the exception being relied on.** The trigger is
  an inline box inside running text: vertical padding grows the target without changing the
  paragraph's leading, but reaching 44 CSS pixels would overlap the line above it and trade
  one motor problem for a worse one. SC 2.5.8 exempts inline targets, and
  [Target size and motor](../accessibility/target-size-and-motor.mdx) asks for the exception
  to be named out loud rather than assumed. The nightly layout run measures every button's
  box and will report this one.

  Two of them can also meet, which is the part nothing will report. Vertical padding on an
  inline box leaves the line box alone, which is why `inline` was chosen. The padding box
  nevertheless still reaches past it above and below, so two terms on adjacent wrapped lines
  sitting roughly over one another have overlapping targets, and a reader with a tremor lands
  on the wrong definition rather than on nothing. SC 2.5.8's exemption covers the size of a
  target and says nothing about two of them overlapping, and the nightly run measures each
  box rather than the space between two, so this sentence is the only place it is recorded.
- **No screen reader has been run against it.** The description-carries-the-collapsed-
  definition technique is the load-bearing claim on this page and it is argued from the
  accessible-name computation, not from a recording of VoiceOver or TalkBack.
- **No speech-input software has been run against the trigger.** The accessible name is
  computed from the button's own content, so it contains the visible word by construction.
  That is an argument about the name computation rather than a recording of Voice Control or
  Voice Access matching it. Related and outside this page:
  [Conformance report](../accessibility/conformance-report.mdx) still records SC 2.5.3 as not
  evaluated on the ground that no labelled control exists, which this component makes untrue.
- **Contrast has not been measured for this component.** The definition uses the muted
  foreground role; that pair is measured at the token layer and not on this surface, and
  `prefers-contrast` and `forced-colors` are not implemented anywhere in opsinjs yet.

## API reference

<PropsTable name="TermProps" />

<PropsTable name="TermGlossaryProviderProps" />

There is deliberately no way to supply a definition at a call site. Definitions live in the
glossary so that they are consistent across every screen, reviewable in one place,
translatable, and countable. A product can ask *how many words are we asking our readers to
learn?* and get an answer. The glossary arrives once, through `TermGlossaryProvider`, as an
array of entries; a context rather than a module-level registry, because a module registry
exists once per module graph and a glossary registered on the server is not the one a client
component sees.

An entry is a `GlossaryEntry`: `id` is the key a call site writes, `word` is the clinical
word spelt the way it appears on the reader's own paperwork, and `plain` is the everyday
meaning. `expansion` is the full form when the word is an abbreviation, `speech` is how the
letters are spoken when synthesis would otherwise read them as a word, and `showBoth`
carries the glossary's own policy for the entry. Only `plain-only` changes what is rendered,
as *Clinical meaning* above sets out. An empty or whitespace-only `plain`, `expansion` or
`speech` is read as an absent field rather than as a value, because an unfilled column in a
spreadsheet export or a half-done translation pass is how a glossary usually acquires one;
an entry whose `plain` is empty renders its word unmarked and warns, rather than printing an
empty pair of brackets after it. The interface is not in the tables above because
`scripts/build-reference.mts` extracts only interfaces whose name ends in `Props`; it is
documented in `registry/bases/base/term.tsx`, beside the code that reads it.

An `id` that is not in the glossary renders unmarked and warns in development. What renders
is `children` where a call site supplied any, and otherwise the raw `id`. So
`<Term id="blood-pressure" />` in an application that has forgotten the provider prints
*blood-pressure* in the middle of a sentence, and `<Term id="egfr" />` prints *egfr*, which
is not the spelling on anybody's paperwork. A hole where the word should be would break the
sentence for every reader rather than only for the reviewer, so the key stays and the
behaviour is written down here instead of being discovered. Marking a word whose explanation
does not exist is the one outcome worse than leaving it plain, and a component cannot fail
somebody else's build from inside a render. The warning is development-only by policy, so in
the build a reader uses there is no signal at all. The build-time half of that promise
belongs in a lint rule over the product's own source, and it does not exist yet.
